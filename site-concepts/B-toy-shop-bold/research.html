Research hub · The Virtual Autism Project

What the research says about screens and early childhood, and what it doesn’t.

“Virtual autism” is a term some clinicians use. It is not a medical diagnosis.

The studies on this page describe associations: patterns seen across large groups of children. An association is not proof that one thing causes another. Nothing here can tell you what is happening with your own child. If you have questions about your child’s development, their doctor is the right person to ask.

What the studies say

Eight sources, in the order they were published. For each one: what it looked at, what it found, and what it cannot tell us.

  1. 2016

    American Academy of Pediatrics, “Media and Young Minds”

    Pediatrics 2016;138(5)

    What it is: a policy statement on media use in early childhood for families and paediatricians.

    Limits: guidance based on reviewing the evidence, not a new study.

  2. 2018

    Paper versus screens for reading

    Delgado P et al., Educational Research Review 2018;25:23–38

    What it found: across 54 studies and more than 171,000 readers, reading comprehension had an advantage on paper over screens.

    Limits: it is about reading comprehension, not about young children’s talk or development.

  3. 2019

    Screen time and developmental screening scores

    Madigan S et al., JAMA Pediatrics 2019;173(3):244–250

    What it found: children with more screen time at ages 2 and 3 tended to score lower on a developmental screening test later on.

    Limits: it shows an association, not a cause.

  4. 2019

    The paper that proposed the term “virtual autism”

    Harlé B, Trends in Neuroscience and Education 2019;17:100119

    What it is: a hypothesis paper that proposed the term.

    Limits: a hypothesis, not a tested finding. It did not establish a diagnosis, a cause or a treatment.

  5. 2020

    Early screen viewing, play and autism-like signs

    Heffler KF et al., JAMA Pediatrics 2020;174(7):690–696

    What it found: early screen viewing was associated with more autism-like signs later in toddlerhood, and more parent–child play with fewer.

    Limits: it shows an association, not a cause.

  6. 2022

    Screen time at age 1 and later diagnosis

    Kushima M et al., JAMA Pediatrics 2022;176(4):384–391

    What it found: longer screen time at age 1 was associated with a later autism diagnosis in boys, but not in girls.

    Limits: an association only; the difference between boys and girls is not explained.

  7. 2023

    Screen time at age 1 and communication at 2 and 4

    Takahashi I et al., JAMA Pediatrics 2023;177(10):1039–1046

    What it found: among 7,097 children, more daily screen time at age 1 was linked, step by step, with more delay in communication and problem-solving at ages 2 and 4.

    Limits: it shows a dose-response association, not a cause.

  8. 2024

    Screen time and the talk around a child

    Brushe ME et al., JAMA Pediatrics 2024;178(4):369–375

    What it found: at 36 months, more screen time was associated with less talk around the child each day:

    1,139
    fewer adult words heard
    843
    fewer child vocalisations
    194
    fewer back-and-forth turns

    Limits: it shows what was measured alongside screen time, not that screens caused it.

Guidelines at a glance

The World Health Organization’s 2019 guidelines for children under 5, drawn on the same age ruler we use in the shop.

For school-age children and classrooms, UNESCO’s Global Education Monitoring Report 2023: Technology in education reviews the evidence on technology in education.

What this is not

  • Not a diagnosis. Only a qualified professional who knows your child can assess their development.
  • Not a treatment. Our books and kits are for reading, talking and playing. They do not treat, cure or prevent any condition.
  • Not a verdict on you. Screens are part of most family days, including ours. There is no shame in that here.
  • Not about any school or product. We summarise research in general terms and never rate a school, company or app.

Questions you might bring to your child’s doctor

  1. How is my child’s talking and understanding coming along for their age?
  2. What should I look out for over the next few months?
  3. Is there anything you would like us to try at home, or anyone you would like us to see?
  4. How do you suggest we think about screens in our family’s day?
