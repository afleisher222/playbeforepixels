The Virtual Autism Project

What large studies have found about screens, talk and development in the first years, what they have not found, and what the guidelines recommend. Written for parents and teachers; every source cited in full.

Please read first

“Virtual autism” is a term some clinicians use. It is not a medical diagnosis.

The studies below describe associations: patterns seen across many children. They do not show that screens cause autism, and nothing here can tell you what is happening with your own child.

If you have questions about your child’s development, talk with your child’s doctor.

01 · The term

What “virtual autism” means, and what it doesn’t

The phrase comes from a 2019 hypothesis paper by a French psychiatrist, Dr Bruno Harlé, describing young children with heavy screen exposure who showed autism-like behaviours. A hypothesis paper proposes an idea for others to test. It is not a trial, and it does not establish a diagnosis.

Since then, a handful of large studies have looked at screen time in the first years alongside language, communication and development. Their findings are summarised below, in plain words and with the limits stated.

What this hub will never say: that screens cause autism, that autism is a parent’s fault, or that anything we sell treats, prevents or reverses any condition.

02 · The studies

What the studies found

In order of publication. Each is observational: it can show a link, not a cause.

  1. 2019
    Hypothesis paper

    A clinician names the pattern

    Describes young children with heavy screen exposure and autism-like symptoms, and proposes the term “virtual autism”.

    Harlé B. Trends in Neuroscience and Education 2019;17:100119.

  2. 2019
    Cohort study

    More screen time, lower developmental screening scores

    In about 2,400 Canadian children, higher screen time at 24 and 36 months was linked to poorer developmental screening results a year later.

    Madigan S et al. JAMA Pediatrics 2019;173(3):244–250.

  3. 2020
    Cohort study

    Screens and play at 12 months

    Screen viewing at 12 months was linked to more autism-like symptoms at age 2. Daily parent–child play was linked to fewer.

    Heffler KF et al. JAMA Pediatrics 2020;174(7):690–696.

  4. 2022
    Cohort study

    A large Japanese cohort

    Longer screen time at age 1 was associated with an autism diagnosis at age 3 in boys. No association was found in girls.

    Kushima M et al. JAMA Pediatrics 2022;176(4):384–391.

  5. 2023
    Cohort study

    Communication and problem-solving

    In 7,097 children, screen time at age 1 showed a dose–response link to communication and problem-solving delays at ages 2 and 4.

    Takahashi I et al. JAMA Pediatrics 2023;177(10):1039–1046.

  6. 2024
    Cohort study

    Screens and talk at home

    Recordings of the language around toddlers found that more screen time went with fewer words from adults and fewer back-and-forth exchanges. At 36 months, the daily difference was:

    1,139fewer adult words
    843fewer child vocalizations
    194fewer conversational turns

    Brushe ME et al. JAMA Pediatrics 2024;178(4):369–375.

03 · Guidelines

What the guidelines recommend, by age

The two guidelines we cite cover children under five. Less is better, and none is recommended for babies.

Under 1
No screen time.
WHO, 2019
Under 18–24 months
Avoid digital media other than video-chatting.
AAP, 2016
Ages 2–4
No more than one hour a day; less is better. If screens are used, choose high-quality programmes and watch together.
WHO, 2019 · AAP, 2016
Ages 1–4
At least 180 minutes a day of active play of any kind, spread through the day.
WHO, 2019
Ages 5–12
These guidelines don’t set a number for school-age children. The classroom research below looks at learning instead.

04 · School

Screens and school

General research for teachers and parent groups. It never names or rates a school, district or product.

  1. 2018
    Meta-analysis

    Reading on paper and on screens

    A review of 54 studies with more than 171,000 readers found better comprehension when reading on paper than on screens, especially for informational text and timed reading.

    Delgado P et al. Educational Research Review 2018;25:23–38.

  2. 2023
    Global report

    Use technology where it helps learning

    UNESCO’s global education report found little strong evidence for much classroom technology and recommends using it when it improves learning.

    UNESCO. Global Education Monitoring Report 2023: Technology in education.

06 · Method

How we read research

What we cite
Peer-reviewed studies and official guidelines only, each read in full, not from a headline.
How we word it
Associations are described as associations. We say what a study did not find as well as what it did.
What we don’t do
Give medical advice, diagnose, or claim that any book or printable treats, prevents or reverses anything.
When we update
This page is reviewed every quarter. Last reviewed September 2026.
