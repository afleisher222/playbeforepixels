The Virtual Autism Project

A plain-language reading room on young children, screens and talk, for parents and teachers. Every claim on this page comes from one of the ten sources listed at the bottom.

Read this first

“Virtual autism” is a term some clinicians use. It is not a medical diagnosis.

The studies below describe associations, not causes. None of them can tell you what is happening with your own child. If you have concerns, your child’s doctor is the right person to ask.

This page is parent education, not medical advice. It doesn’t sell anything.

What “virtual autism” means, and what it doesn’t.

The phrase was put forward in a 2019 hypothesis paper to describe young children with heavy early screen exposure who showed some autism-like behaviours. A hypothesis is an idea to be tested. It is not a finding, and it is not a diagnosis a doctor can give.

What it is

  • A term some clinicians use
  • An idea from a 2019 hypothesis paper
  • A prompt to look at how much talk and play a day holds

What it isn’t

  • A medical diagnosis
  • Proof that screens cause autism
  • Something an online checklist can tell you about your child

The studies, in plain words.

Six papers come up again and again, starting with the one that coined the term. Each card says what the researchers looked at, what they found, and what the study can’t tell us.

  1. Trends in Neuroscience and Education · 2019Hypothesis

    Harlé B

    Looked at
    Clinical observations of young children with heavy early screen exposure.
    Proposed
    The idea of “virtual autism”.
    Can’t tell us
    Anything as a finding. It proposes an idea; it doesn’t test one.

  2. JAMA Pediatrics · 2019Screening scores

    Madigan S et al.

    Looked at
    Screen time at ages 2 and 3, and scores on a developmental screening test at 3 and 5.
    Found
    More screen time was associated with lower screening scores later on.
    Can’t tell us
    A screening score is not a diagnosis, and families differ in many ways besides screens.

  3. JAMA Pediatrics · 2020Play and symptoms

    Heffler KF et al.

    Looked at
    Screen viewing and parent–child play at 12 months, and autism-like symptoms at age 2.
    Found
    Screen viewing at 12 months was associated with more autism-like symptoms; daily reading or play with a parent was associated with fewer. No link was found with an autism diagnosis itself.
    Can’t tell us
    Whether screens caused the symptoms.

  4. JAMA Pediatrics · 2022Boys and girls

    Kushima M et al.

    Looked at
    Screen time at age 1 and autism diagnosed by age 3, in a large birth cohort.
    Found
    Longer screen time at 1 was associated with autism at 3 in boys, but not in girls.
    Can’t tell us
    Why the link appeared in boys only, or whether it is causal.

  5. JAMA Pediatrics · 20237,097 children

    Takahashi I et al.

    Looked at
    Screen time at age 1 and development at ages 2 and 4.
    Found
    The more screen time at 1, the more often children showed delays in communication and problem-solving at 2 and 4.
    Can’t tell us
    What the screen time replaced, or what it would mean for one particular child.

  6. JAMA Pediatrics · 2024Talk at home

    Brushe ME et al.

    Looked at
    Screen time and the talk around children at home, at 36 months.
    Found
    More screen time was associated with less talk every day:

    1,139fewer adult words

    843fewer child vocalisations

    194fewer back-and-forth turns

    Can’t tell us
    Whether the talk was lost because of screens or for other reasons.

WHO and AAP guidance, side by side.

Two public-health bodies, two slightly different lines, one shared direction: very little screen time in the first years, and lots of talk and active play.

Screen time guidance by age
AgeWorld Health Organization, 2019American Academy of Pediatrics, 2016
Under 1No screen time.Avoid screen media other than video-chatting until 18 months.
1–4 yearsAt least 180 minutes a day of active play, spread through the day.Unplugged play and time together every day.
2–4 yearsNo more than 1 hour a day; less is better.From 2 to 5: up to 1 hour a day of high-quality programmes, watched together.

Paper or screen for reading?

For schools, UNESCO’s 2023 Global Education Monitoring Report on technology in education asks for technology to be used where it clearly helps learning, and not simply because it is available.

None of this makes screens bad or paper magic. It’s a reason to keep books, paper and conversation in the mix, at home and in class.

If you’re worried about your child.

Nothing on this page, or any website, can tell you whether your child is autistic. Here’s what you can do instead.

  1. Talk to your child’s doctor. Bring a few notes on what you’ve noticed: words, gestures, pointing, eye contact, how your child plays.
  2. Ask about a free evaluation. In the US, every state runs an early intervention program for children under 3, and you can contact it yourself without a referral. From age 3, your local public school district can evaluate. Elsewhere, your doctor or health visitor can point you to local services.
  3. Keep talking and playing. Back-and-forth talk and play are good for every child. They are not a treatment and not a substitute for getting advice.
