Hello

A note before you start

This book is for ordinary days: rainy mornings, long afternoons and the ten minutes before dinner. You don’t need special toys, a craft cupboard or a perfect plan. Most of these plays use a cup, a box, a sock or nothing at all.

You don’t need to do all 100, either. Pick one that fits your child’s age and your energy today. Try it, talk while you play, and see what your child does with it. Some plays will be a hit and some will flop. Both are fine.

The best part is the back-and-forth. You say something, your child answers with a look, a sound or a word, and you answer back. That’s the whole idea.

Have fun, and go easy on yourself.

Talk while you play

Six easy moves

Every play has a talk line tagged with one of these moves. They are plain, everyday ways to take turns talking. There is nothing to memorize: pick one and try it today.

1

Pause and wait

Say a little, then stop. Count to five in your head and give your child room to answer with a look, a sound, a word or a gesture.

“Ready, set… (wait) …go!”
2

Say what you see

Put words to what your child is looking at or doing, right as it happens. No questions needed.

“You’re stirring. Stir, stir, stir.”
3

Repeat and add one

When your child says or shows something, say it back and add one more word: "Ball." "Big ball!"

Child: “Ball.” You: “Red ball!”
4

Offer a choice

Hold up two options and wait. Pointing, reaching and looking all count as answers.

“Bubbles or blocks?”
5

Follow their lead

Let your child choose what to do and how. Join in, copy them and talk about their idea.

“Oh, Teddy goes in the box! In he goes.”
6

Sing and gesture

Songs, rhythm and simple hand moves make words easy to join in with. Repeat the same ones often.

“Wave bye-bye… bye-bye, bath!”

Any answer counts: a look, a smile, a point, a sound, a sign, a tap on a talking device or a word. If your child doesn’t answer, that’s fine too. Say the word yourself, smile and keep playing.

Talk, sing and read in the language you know best. Every language counts.

Safety first

The toilet-paper tube test

For children under 3: if something fits through a toilet-paper tube (about 1.25 in or 3.2 cm across), it is too small to play with. Check toys, lids, food and anything a big sibling leaves out.

  • A grown-up is always right there. Every play in this book is for doing together, not for leaving a child alone with.
  • No balloons for children under 8. Uninflated or popped balloons are a choking risk.
  • No cords or strings long enough to wrap around a neck. That includes blind and curtain cords, ribbons, costume ties and charger cables.
  • Water: stay within arm’s reach. Even shallow water, even for a moment. Tip out tubs, buckets and bowls as soon as play ends.
  • Food: soft, small and sitting down. No whole grapes, nuts, popcorn, raw apple or hard candy for toddlers. Cut food into small, soft pieces.
  • Button batteries and small magnets stay locked away. Swallowing one is an emergency. Keep battery covers screwed shut.
  • Outdoors: traffic, sun and water first. Stay where you can see each other. No berries, mushrooms or small stones in hands or mouths.
  • Sleep safely. Tummy time only while your baby is awake and watched. Babies sleep on their backs, in a clear crib.
In an emergency call your local emergency number (911 in the US). If you think your child has swallowed something harmful, call Poison Control (1-800-222-1222 in the US) or your local poison center. Ages in this book are a guide: if a play does not feel safe for your child, skip it or change it.

The why, in plain words

Why play? Why talk?

You don’t need to be a teacher or buy anything special. Everyday play and talk, a few minutes at a time, is something every family can do.

Back-and-forth is the heart of it

Taking turns (you say something, your child answers with a look, a sound or a word, and you answer back) is the simplest thing in this book, and the one we come back to on every page. A 2024 study linked more screen time at age 3 with fewer adult words, fewer child sounds and fewer back-and-forth turns at home each day (Brushe and colleagues, JAMA Pediatrics). That is a link, not proof that screens cause it. It is a good reminder that everyday conversation is worth making room for.

Moving counts

The World Health Organization (2019) recommends that children aged 1 to 4 spend at least 180 minutes a day in a variety of physical activities, spread through the day. Babies do well with plenty of floor play, including tummy time while awake. Look for plays marked Big energy in the Quick finder.

Screens, gently

The same WHO guidelines say that sedentary screen time is not recommended for babies under 1 or for 1-year-olds, and that for 2- to 4-year-olds it should be no more than 1 hour a day, with less being better. Real life has screens in it. Turn to When screens are on anyway (page 81) for friendly, practical ideas.

This book is parent education, not medical advice. If you ever have questions about how your child is growing, moving or talking, ask your child’s doctor. In the US, every state runs a free early intervention program for babies and toddlers, and your child’s doctor can point you to it.

Before you start

Set up for easy play

A little setup makes play easy to start on a busy day. None of this needs buying: look in your cupboards and recycling first.

A play basket

Keep a basket of everyday play things within easy reach. When you need a play fast, it’s already there. See the list for each age below.

Rotate, don’t buy

Keep 6 to 8 toys out and put the rest away. Swap a few each week. Old toys feel new again.

A "yes" space

One safe corner where everything in reach is fine to touch, so you can say "yes" more and "no" less.

Bored is OK

Children don’t need entertaining every minute. Playing alone nearby while you cook or rest counts as play too.

Face to face

No prep No mess Plays about 5 min Indoors · Wind-down
You need: Nothing but you Nothing to buy

Hold your baby about a foot from your face. Slowly open your mouth wide, stick out your tongue or raise your eyebrows, then stop and watch. Give your baby time to look, wriggle or try it back.

Make it easier: Just smile and wait. A slow blink or a smile back is a turn.

Make it harder: Add a sound to each face: "ahh" with a wide mouth, "ooh" with round lips.

Talk while you play · Pause and wait
“You’re looking at me! Hello, you.”
Safety: Support your baby’s head and neck. If your baby turns away or fusses, rest and try again later.

Tummy-time mirror chat

No prep No mess Plays about 5 min Indoors
You need: a blanket, an unbreakable baby mirror

Lay your baby on their tummy on a blanket on the floor. Lie down facing them at eye level and prop the mirror to one side. Chat, smile and point to the baby in the mirror.

Make it easier: Lie back and rest your baby on your chest, facing you, for a minute or two.

Make it harder: Slide the mirror a little further away so your baby reaches and pivots toward it.

Talk while you play · Say what you see
“Who’s that? That’s you!”
Safety: Tummy time only while your baby is awake and you are watching. Babies always sleep on their backs.

Name-it walk

No prep No mess Plays about 5 min Indoors
You need: Nothing but you Nothing to buy

Carry your baby around your home. Stop at things your baby looks at, touch them together and name them: light, door, window, plant.

No guilt, just ideas

When screens are on anyway

Screens are part of family life. This book isn’t about being perfect. It’s about adding more play and talk around the screen time you already have.

1
Watch together when you can.
Sit with your child, talk about what you see, sing the songs and link it to real life: "A red bus! Like the one we saw."
2
Choose slow and simple.
Calmer shows with one story at a time are easier to talk about than fast, busy ones.
3
Background TV off.
When nobody is really watching, switch it off. It’s hard to talk and play over it.
4
Warn, then land.
"Two more minutes, then Pillow Mountain!" Have the next play ready so the switch feels like a treat.
5
Give screens a spot in the day.
A regular time, say while dinner cooks, that doesn’t grow or shrink with chores or behavior. Meals and the hour before bed make easy screen-free spots.
6
Video calls are different.
A call with Grandma is a real back-and-forth conversation. Help your child wave, show and tell.
7
Give phones a spot too.
A basket by the door during play time makes it easier for everyone to join in.
8
Tomorrow is a new day.
A show while you shower or cook is ordinary life. Pick one play tomorrow and carry on.
What the guidelines say

World Health Organization (2019): sedentary screen time is not recommended for babies under 1 or 1-year-olds; for 2- to 4-year-olds, no more than 1 hour a day, and less is better.

American Academy of Pediatrics (2016): for children younger than 18 months, avoid screen media other than video-chatting; for ages 2 to 5, limit screen use to 1 hour a day of high-quality programming, ideally watched together.

Even the tablet likes a rest. Sometimes.
