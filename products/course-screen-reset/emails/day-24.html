Grown-up phones

Children notice our screens too. A phone parking spot and a few spoken habits help the whole family.

This lesson is about us, the grown-ups, and it comes with zero guilt. Phones are where our work, our bills, our friends and our news live. Of course we check them.

Children notice, though. When a grown-up looks at a phone, conversation often pauses, and young children may not know why.

Three small habits help.

A phone parking spot. Choose a place (a shelf, a basket, a drawer) where your phone lives during play time, meals and the first ten minutes after you reunite. Out of sight, out of hand.

Say it out loud. When you do need your phone, tell your child what you’re doing: “I’m checking the bus time. Done.” It shows that phones are tools, and you come back.

Pick one phone-free window. Dinner, bath time or the bedtime routine. Just one, every day.

You don’t need to be perfect. Children learn more from how we come back to them than from how often we get distracted.

Today’s one small step: Choose a phone parking spot and one phone-free window every day.

Today’s play: Look up together

From 18 months · No prep · No mess · Quick play
You need: Nothing but you

Put your phone in its parking spot. Lie on the floor or grass side by side and look up at the ceiling, the sky or the clouds. Say what you see. Take turns finding shapes.

Talk while you play · Say what you see
“I see a cloud like a… dog! A big fluffy dog. (wait)”

Make it easier: Lie together and point at the light, the fan, the window.
Make it harder: Preschoolers find shapes in the clouds or invent star pictures and give them names.
Tired-grown-up version (2 minutes): Lie still and listen: what sounds can you hear?

Safety: Lie on a clean, flat surface. Outside, check the ground first and never look straight at the sun.

Plain words for: When your child sees you on your phone

“I’m checking the weather for tomorrow. Done! Phone’s going in its spot.”

“Give me one minute to finish this message, then I’m all yours.”

Naming what you’re doing and coming back is the habit children copy.
