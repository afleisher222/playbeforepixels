# Follow their lead

**When the child chooses the play and the grown-up joins in, children often talk and play more.**

Today, let your child be the boss of the play.

For ten minutes, whatever your child wants to play, you join in. If they line up cars, you line up cars. If they put a teddy to bed, you whisper “goodnight, teddy”. If they bang a pot, you bang a pot, and then wait.

It sounds simple, but it’s hard for grown-ups. We want to teach, correct, improve: “Cars go on the road, not the table.” Today, resist. Their way is the right way for these ten minutes.

When you follow a child’s lead, you are talking about exactly what they are interested in, right when they’re interested. That’s often when the most talk happens.

Get down to their level, face to face if you can. Copy what they do. Say what you see. Pause and wait. You’re using all this week’s moves at once.

Big kids love this too. Let them teach you a game they made up, the rules of a sport or how to draw their favorite character.

**Today’s one small step:** Give your child ten minutes to lead the play. Copy, comment and wait.

## Today’s play: You’re the boss

*From 12 months · No prep · A little mess · Short play · You need: whatever your child chooses*

Tell your child, “You’re the boss for ten minutes. What should we play?” Then do what they do, the way they do it. Copy their sounds and actions and describe what’s happening.

**Talk while you play (Follow their lead):** “You put teddy in bed. Night-night, teddy. Shhh.”

- **Make it easier:** Copy your baby or toddler’s sounds and actions. They’ll often do it again to see you copy.
- **Make it harder:** Big kids teach you a game with their own rules. You follow them exactly.
- **Tired-grown-up version (2 minutes):** Say, “Show me what you’re doing,” and watch with interest.
- **Safety:** Stay close, and redirect gently if the play turns unsafe: “Climbing is for the floor cushions.”

## Plain words for: When their play seems “wrong”

> (Instead of fixing it) “The cars are on the table! They’re having a party?”
>
> “Show me how it works.”

Curiosity instead of correction keeps your child talking and in charge.
