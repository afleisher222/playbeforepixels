Books, paper and pages

Sharing books and paper together is one of the easiest ways to add talk to the day.

Today: books and paper.

Reading together doesn’t have to mean reading every word. With young children, “reading” can mean pointing at pictures, naming things, making the animal sounds, and letting your child turn the pages whenever they like. Talk about the pictures. Wait for your child to point. Follow their lead to the page they love.

Read the same book again and again if your child asks. Repetition is how children learn a story well enough to join in.

Paper books have one more thing going for them. A review of 54 studies found that readers tended to understand informational text a little better on paper than on screens (Delgado and colleagues, 2018). Those studies were with older readers, not toddlers, but whatever the format, reading together is a chance to talk.

Paper play counts too: drawing, scribbling, making lists, writing cards to grandparents, folding paper boats.

Today’s play: Read it your way

From 6 months · No prep · No mess · Short play
You need: 2–3 books

Let your child choose the book and the page. Skip the words if you like and talk about the pictures instead. Follow their finger, name what they point at, make the sounds.

Talk while you play · Follow their lead
“Oh, the dog! Woof! Where’s the dog going?”

Make it easier: One board book, one picture at a time.
Make it harder: Preschoolers “read” the pictures to you, or tell the story to a teddy or a younger sibling.
Tired-grown-up version (2 minutes): Read one short book with the lights low.

Safety: Use sturdy board books with babies and toddlers.

Plain words for: When they want the same book again (and again)

“Again? Yes! This is a good one.”

“This time, you say the last word.”

Repeating is how children learn to join in. Turn repeats into turns.
