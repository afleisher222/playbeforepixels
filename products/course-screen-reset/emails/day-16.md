# Say what you see

**Describing what your child is doing gives them words without asking them to perform.**

A lot of grown-up talk to children is questions: “What color is that? What does a cow say? What’s this?” Questions have their place, but too many can feel like a test, and children often go quiet.

Try this instead: say what you see. Like a friendly sports commentator, put words to what your child is doing right now. “You’re stacking. Up, up. The red one’s on top. Oh, it wobbled!”

You aren’t asking for anything back. You’re giving your child the words for their own actions at the exact moment they care about them. That’s when words stick.

Keep your sentences short, a little longer than your child’s own. If your child uses single words, use two or three. If they use short sentences, add a little more.

Big kids enjoy this too, in a different way: “You’ve been working on that drawing a long time. You added a door.” It tells them you’re paying attention.

**Today’s one small step:** For five minutes, narrate your child’s play without asking a single question.

## Today’s play: Play commentator

*From 12 months · No prep · No mess · Short play · You need: blocks, cups or boxes*

Sit beside your child while they build or play. Describe what they do, like a friendly sports commentator. Stay on their level and keep your hands mostly still: it’s their game.

**Talk while you play (Say what you see):** “Another block… and another… it’s so tall! Crash!”

- **Make it easier:** Use single words: “Up. Up. Crash!”
- **Make it harder:** Big kids commentate your building, or you both commentate a “match” between teddies.
- **Tired-grown-up version (2 minutes):** Commentate from the couch: “And she’s walking to the basket…”
- **Safety:** For under-3s, blocks and cups must be too big to fit through a toilet-paper tube.

## Plain words for: Instead of quizzing

> Instead of “What color is that?” try “You picked the red one.”
>
> Instead of “What’s this?” try “A spoon. You’re banging the spoon.”

Commenting keeps talk flowing; quizzing often stops it.
