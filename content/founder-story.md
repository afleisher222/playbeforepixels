## LONG (about 600 words)

It was late one night when I typed a question I hadn't said out loud to anyone.

Are the videos my daughter loves affecting her development? Are they making her autistic?

I want to be honest about that moment, because I think a lot of parents have one like it. I wasn't looking for someone to blame. I was tired, and I was wondering. My daughter loved those videos, and I had never thought twice about them. That night, I did.

I'm a mom, and I'm also an educator. I thought I knew a fair amount about how young children learn. It turns out that knowing about children and raising one are two different kinds of knowing.

The search results showed me a phrase I had never heard: "virtual autism."

I want to be careful with that term, because it's easy to misread. It's a term some clinicians use. It is not a medical diagnosis, and it doesn't mean screens cause autism. No search result can tell you what's going on with your own child. If you have concerns, talk with your child's doctor, not a search bar late at night.

But that phrase opened a door to research on early screen exposure that no one had ever mentioned to me. So I became a student again. I read the studies themselves, not just the headlines about them. Some were large and careful. Some were small. A lot of it is still being worked out, and researchers disagree about what much of it means. I didn't find a simple verdict, and I've stopped expecting one.

What I did find was a question I couldn't shake: why did I have to go looking for this on my own?

I also came away wanting more of the ordinary things I'd always believed in as an educator: talk, touch, play and paper. So we changed our routines at home. Not everything, and not overnight.

We talked more. There were more hugs and more hands-on play. More paper and crayons came out. We read more books, and then the same books again. We spent more time outside.

Our home got louder. It got messier. It also got closer, in a way that's hard to put into words. More questions, from all of us. More time with our heads close together.

That's the change I can honestly tell you about: how our days feel. It isn't a before-and-after, and it isn't a promise about anyone's child.

I'm not writing this to tell you to throw anything away, and I'm not writing it to make you feel bad about whatever is playing in your house right now. Screens have a place in a lot of real, loving homes. You are doing a hard job with the time and energy you have.

Here's the part that still bothers me. I have training, and I know how to read research. Even so, I had to piece all of this together alone, one search at a time. Most parents don't have hours to dig through studies. Most teachers don't either, even though they're often the people parents turn to first.

That's why I built Play Before Pixels. It's the place I wish I'd found that night: plain-language resources for home and the classroom, with honest notes on what the research does and doesn't say. No scare tactics. No scolding. Nobody counting your minutes.

If you're here late at night with a question you haven't said out loud yet, I understand. Stay a while. Look around. Start wherever you are.

— Founded by a parent and educator

## Compliance changes (summary)

- The show and its creator are never named. Descriptions of the show's style ("gentle and cheerful," "the songs, the faces, the bright, gentle voice") were removed because they could identify it. This avoids implying that a real creator's content causes autism (disparagement and defamation risk).
- No search engine, platform, device, school, district, employer or agency is named. "Googled" is replaced by "searched" or "typed."
- The child has no name, age detail, diagnosis, evaluation or therapy. "Babies and toddlers" became "young children." Anything the child is quoted saying ("read it again") was removed because it could read as a speech outcome.
- There are no health or efficacy claims. "How ordinary the good stuff sounded" was reframed as the founder's own preference, so the copy doesn't imply the research endorses these activities as a fix. Added: "It isn't a before-and-after, and it isn't a promise about anyone's child."
- "Virtual autism" is always framed as "a term some clinicians use," "not a medical diagnosis," and "doesn't mean screens cause autism." Removed: "nothing I read showed that they do," which was an unsupported claim about the research.
- Fear and shame: "scared" became "wondering" or "tired." There's no urgency and no guilt. Unconfirmed claims about the founder's current home ("Screens are still part of our life," "I still put a show on") were replaced with a general line saying screens have a place in many loving homes.
- Invented specifics were removed: 11 p.m., couch, kitchen table, printouts, cold tea, blocks basket, books by the door, salt, muddy shoes, "some of them twice," taking notes.
