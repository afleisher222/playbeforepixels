# What is "virtual autism"? What the research says (and doesn't)

> "Virtual autism" is a term some clinicians use for autism-like behaviors seen in some young children with heavy early screen exposure. It is not a medical diagnosis. Studies have found links (associations) between early screen time and later outcomes, not proof that screens cause autism, and autism is not a parent's fault. If you're worried about your child, talk with your pediatrician and ask about a free early intervention evaluation.

> **Worried? You don't need to wait.**
> 1. Talk with your pediatrician or family doctor.
> 2. Ask for a free evaluation. In the US you can ask your state's early intervention program yourself. [How to ask](/research/early-intervention/).
> 3. Bring [these questions](/research/pediatrician-questions/).
>
> **This is not medical advice.** This page explains research. It can't tell you whether your child is autistic, has a delay, or is developing as expected. Only a professional who meets your child can.

## The short version

- **It is not a diagnosis.** "Virtual autism" is not in the World Health Organization's ICD or the DSM, and even the psychologist who coined it says it is not a medical diagnosis ([Zamfir 2018](/research/studies/zamfir-2018/) [VERIFY]).
- **Where it comes from.** The evidence that uses the label is case reports, small clinic series without comparison groups, and surveys at one point in time. We have found no randomized trials, and no long-term studies designed to test whether signs change when screens are reduced ([Ozyazici 2026](/research/studies/ozyazici-2026/) [VERIFY]).
- **Larger studies find links, not causes, and not always the same link.** Some large studies find that more early screen time goes with somewhat higher scores on autism screening checklists ([Heffler 2020](/research/studies/heffler-2020/)), or with higher odds of an autism diagnosis in some groups ([Kushima 2022](/research/studies/kushima-2022/), boys only). Another large study found no simple "more screens, more autism" pattern ([Melchior 2022](/research/studies/melchior-2022-elfe/) [VERIFY]).
- **But the links may run the other way, or come from something else.** Children with a higher genetic likelihood of autism already watch more as toddlers ([Takahashi N 2023](/research/studies/takahashi-n-2023-genetics/) [VERIFY]). The largest review we have found reported an overall positive link, but it shrank and stopped being statistically significant once the authors corrected for publication bias ([Ophir 2023](/research/studies/ophir-2023-meta-analysis/) [VERIFY]). Two further studies built to get closer to cause found no clear causal signal, though each has important limits: one rests on a debatable statistical assumption, and the other cannot test screen use in infancy ([Lin 2025](/research/studies/lin-2025-lsac/) [VERIFY]; [Cai 2025](/research/studies/cai-2025-mendelian-randomization/) [VERIFY]). None of this rules out that heavy screen use plays some part for some children. It means that has not been shown.
- **Researchers, clinicians and autism organizations have objected to the term.** They say it risks bringing back the old, false idea that parents cause autism, and that it blurs what autism is ([Krijnen 2026](/research/studies/krijnen-2026-autism/) [VERIFY]; [autismus Deutschland](/research/studies/autismus-deutschland-statement/) [VERIFY]).
- **What helps right now does not depend on settling the debate.** If you're worried, ask your pediatrician and ask for a free evaluation. Talking, playing and reading together are good for every child, whatever the research eventually shows.

**How we wrote this:** every claim links to a study page that says exactly what we read. Sources marked **[VERIFY]** are ones we have not yet been able to check against the original paper. Read them as provisional. [Our editorial policy](/research/editorial-policy/) explains how we choose sources, who reviews them, and how to report a mistake.

## A note to autistic readers and their families

If you are autistic, or you love someone who is, parts of this page may be hard to read. Much of the research below treats autism as something to be counted and explained, and the term "virtual autism" has been criticized for the hurt it can cause. Here is where we stand.

- **Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental difference, not damage and not a failure.** Autistic children and adults are whole people. Nothing on this page is meant to suggest that an autistic child is a bad outcome, or that autism is something to be prevented or fixed.
- **Support needs differ.** Some autistic children need a lot of support with communication, daily life, sleep or health. Others need much less. Both are real, and every family's experience deserves respect.
- **Autism is not caused by parenting.** The old idea that parents cause autism was wrong, and autism organizations and researchers warn that "virtual autism" risks bringing it back ([autismus Deutschland](/research/studies/autismus-deutschland-statement/) [VERIFY]; [Krijnen 2026](/research/studies/krijnen-2026-autism/) [VERIFY]). No study has shown that screens cause autism.
- **Screens can meet real needs.** An autistic researcher [VERIFY from the paper itself that the author identifies as autistic; if not confirmed, write "A researcher" and drop "their own"] describes how screen use met their own needs for predictability, safety, autonomy and belonging, and argues that research should ask how and why autistic people use media and weigh benefits as well as risks ([van Asselt 2026](/research/studies/van-asselt-2026/) [VERIFY]).
- **A communication device is a voice, not "screen time".** Tablets and phones used for AAC (augmentative and alternative communication) are communication tools. If your child uses one, keep it within reach.
- **Research words are not our words for people.** Studies use clinical terms such as "disorder", "symptoms", "severity" and "risk". We quote them when we describe what a study measured, and we don't use them to describe autistic people. The [glossary](/research/glossary/) explains what they mean.
- **Language.** Many autistic people prefer identity-first language ("autistic child"), and we mostly use it here. Some people and families prefer "child with autism", and that is their choice.
- **Autistic voices belong in this debate.** We give critiques by autistic researchers and statements from autistic-led organizations the same care as the clinical studies, and we are still searching for more of them [VERIFY: name only authors whose papers say they are autistic]. Our [editorial policy](/research/editorial-policy/) requires a paid autistic sensitivity read before any research page is published.
- **If your child has just been identified as autistic:** your child is the same child they were yesterday. Support is about what helps your child communicate, feel safe and thrive, not about making them seem less autistic. Autistic adults' own writing and autistic-led organizations are good places to learn what autism is like from the inside.

If anything here is hurtful, wrong or missing, please [tell us](/research/editorial-policy/#corrections).

## Where the term came from

**Romania, 2018.** Psychologist Marius Teodor Zamfir looked back at the case histories of young children newly diagnosed with autism at two private centres. According to their parents' recollection, many had spent more than four hours a day with screens before age 3. He called this pattern "autism virtual" and proposed that it resembled childhood autism. The paper appeared in a literary-studies journal, not a medical one. The children it describes **already had autism diagnoses**. [Read our summary](/research/studies/zamfir-2018/) [VERIFY].

**France, 2017 to 2019.** In 2017, warnings from a French maternal and child health physician, including a widely shared video, set off public alarm that heavy tablet and phone use could produce an autism-like picture in toddlers ([summary](/research/studies/ecrans-et-autisme-2017-handout/) [VERIFY]). In 2018, three French clinicians proposed a different name, "EPEE" (early and excessive screen exposure), which deliberately avoids the word autism ([Marcelli 2018](/research/studies/marcelli-2018-epee/) [VERIFY]). In 2019, French child psychiatrist Bruno Harlé published a hypothesis paper arguing that intensive early screen exposure might play a part in autism-like signs in some young children, and proposed the label "virtual autism" ([Harlé 2019](/research/studies/harle-2019/)). His paper is an argument for an idea, built from case reports. It does not test the idea.

**Earlier roots.** A 2008 US study noticed that rainier counties had higher autism rates and speculated about indoor TV time, but it had no information on any child's screen use ([Waldman 2008](/research/studies/waldman-2008/) [VERIFY]). In 2016 two US researchers proposed an untested model in which heavy early audiovisual stimulation competes with social brain development ([Heffler and Oestreicher 2016](/research/studies/heffler-oestreicher-2016/) [VERIFY]).

**Other labels you may see.** "Digital autism", "electronic screen syndrome" ([Dunckley](/research/studies/dunckley-electronic-screen-syndrome/) [VERIFY]), "post-digital nannying autism syndrome" ([Pouretemad 2022](/research/studies/pouretemad-2022-pdnas/) [VERIFY]), and in other languages "autisme virtuel", "virtueller Autismus", "autismul virtual" and "sanal otizm". None is a recognized diagnosis. Some critics suggest neutral terms such as "screen-related developmental delay" ([Krijnen 2026](/research/studies/krijnen-2026-autism/) [VERIFY]). See the [glossary](/research/glossary/).

## What studies have found

It helps to sort the research by **how** it was done, because some designs can answer the question much better than others.

### Case reports and small clinic series

A few reports describe young children with heavy screen use and autism-like signs whose developmental scores changed after screen time was cut and adult time added ([Heffler 2022](/research/studies/heffler-2022-case-report/), two children [VERIFY]; [an uncontrolled series of 30 children in Pakistan](/research/studies/apims-2023-pakistan/) [VERIFY]; [a very small series in Indonesia](/research/studies/hermawati-2018/) [VERIFY]).

These reports matter because they are where the idea comes from. But they **cannot tell us why** a child changed. None had a comparison group. In each, less screen time came together with more adult attention and, often, professional support, and young children develop quickly anyway. Assessors usually knew what had changed at home. And a lower score on an autism checklist is not the same as a child being happier or better off.

### Surveys at one point in time, and case-control studies

Many studies compare screen use in autistic and non-autistic children, or measure screen time and autism-screening scores at the same time. Autistic toddlers often started screens earlier and watched more ([Chonchaiya 2011](/research/studies/chonchaiya-2011/) [VERIFY]; [Tunisia 2025](/research/studies/tunisia-2025-screen-patterns/) [VERIFY]). In 5,336 Singapore toddlers, above-average screen time went with about 24% higher odds of a positive autism screen ([Sundarimaa 2025](/research/studies/sundarimaa-2025-singapore/) [VERIFY]). A positive screen is not a diagnosis.

Not every survey finds a difference: in a large US national survey of school-age children, autistic and non-autistic children had similar total screen time ([Montes 2016](/research/studies/montes-2016/) [VERIFY]).

These designs **cannot tell which came first**. In the Tunisian study, autistic children often preferred repetitive videos and looked at the screen from the side. Those are ways of watching that fit with autism already being there.

### Studies that follow children over time

- **United States, 2,152 children.** TV or video at 12 months went with slightly higher autism-checklist scores at age 2 (4.2% higher), but **not** with screening "at risk". Daily parent-child play went with lower scores ([Heffler 2020](/research/studies/heffler-2020/)). Independent experts criticized the crude screen measure and pointed out that no one was diagnosed ([Science Media Centre](/research/studies/smc-2020-expert-reaction/) [VERIFY]).
- **Japan, 84,030 children.** In **boys but not girls**, longer screen time at age 1 went with higher odds of an autism diagnosis by age 3. The odds were higher in each group from 1 hour a day upward, but the estimates for 2 to 4 hours and for 4 hours or more were similar, with widely overlapping ranges, so the study cannot tell whether the odds keep rising above about 2 hours. Autism was recorded only up to age 3 ([Kushima 2022](/research/studies/kushima-2022/)).
- **France, 12,950 two-year-olds.** Screen use went with very slightly higher odds of a *medium* screening score but *lower* odds of a *high-risk* score. There was no simple "more screens, more autism" pattern ([Melchior 2022](/research/studies/melchior-2022-elfe/) [VERIFY]).
- **Australia, 5,107 children.** More than 14 hours a week at age 2 went with autism by 12 in a standard analysis, but not in a second analysis designed to get closer to cause, which itself rests on a debatable assumption ([Lin 2025](/research/studies/lin-2025-lsac/) [VERIFY]).
- **United States, 82 toddlers, many with a family history of autism or ADHD.** Children later identified with autism or ADHD concerns had more screen time at 18 months. The study cannot tell whether that screen time was an early sign or a contributor ([Hill 2024](/research/studies/hill-2024/) [VERIFY]).

### Reviews that combine many studies

- The **largest** review we have found pooled 46 studies with 562,131 people. It found a positive link overall, but also strong signs of **publication bias**: studies with positive findings were more likely to be published. After a standard correction, the link was much smaller and **no longer statistically significant**. The authors concluded that the proclaimed association is "not sufficiently supported" ([Ophir 2023](/research/studies/ophir-2023-meta-analysis/) [VERIFY]).
- Two other reviews report a link ([Yuan](/research/studies/yuan-jadd-systematic-review/) [VERIFY]; [Liu 2025](/research/studies/liu-2025-meta-analysis/) [VERIFY]). Whether they corrected for publication bias is not yet confirmed. Another found the evidence mixed and too thin to confirm any link ([Sarfraz 2023](/research/studies/sarfraz-2023-systematic-review/) [VERIFY]).

### Studies that test other explanations

- **Genes.** In Japan, children with a higher genetic likelihood of autism already spent more time on screens as toddlers. The authors suggest long screen time may be an **early sign** rather than a cause ([Takahashi N 2023](/research/studies/takahashi-n-2023-genetics/) [VERIFY]). In older US children, shared genetic risk explained part of the link between screen time and attention and emotional problems ([Zhang 2023](/research/studies/zhang-2023-shared-genetic-risk/) [VERIFY]).
- **Natural experiments with genes.** A Mendelian randomization study found no evidence that genetically predicted leisure screen time affects autism. Its genetic markers reflect mostly adult screen habits, so it cannot test infant exposure directly ([Cai 2025](/research/studies/cai-2025-mendelian-randomization/) [VERIFY]).

### Screens and talk: related research that is *not* about autism

A separate body of research looks at screens and everyday talk and development. It is often mixed up with the autism question, so we keep it apart:

- More screen time at age 1 went with higher odds of communication and problem-solving delays on a screening questionnaire at 2 and 4 ([Takahashi I 2023](/research/studies/takahashi-i-2023/), 7,097 children).
- In home audio recordings, screen time at 36 months went with less talk. Scaled to the average amount of daily screen time in the study, that was 1,139 fewer adult words, 843 fewer child vocalizations and 194 fewer back-and-forth conversational turns a day ([Brushe 2024](/research/studies/brushe-2024/)).
- Background TV went with weaker language, and watching *together* went with stronger language ([Madigan 2020](/research/studies/madigan-2020-language-meta-analysis/) [VERIFY]; [Mallawaarachchi 2024](/research/studies/mallawaarachchi-2024-contexts/) [VERIFY]).

These are associations too. They are **not autism studies**, and we never present them as evidence about autism.

## What critics say

Criticism of the term started almost as soon as the term did:

- **Romania.** A Romanian psychology site called the original study an essay lacking scientific rigor, not published in a recognized scientific journal ([summary](/research/studies/psihologia-ro-critique/) [VERIFY]).
- **France, 2017-18.** French commentators called the viral alert subjective and without scientific foundation ([summary](/research/studies/psychologiescientifique-critique/) [VERIFY]).
- **UK, 2020.** Independent researchers said the 2020 US study's screen measure was crude and that no child was diagnosed ([Science Media Centre](/research/studies/smc-2020-expert-reaction/) [VERIFY]). A media and disability researcher published a letter calling for better research ([Alper 2020](/research/studies/alper-2020-letter/) [VERIFY]).
- **Germany.** The national autism association says the term is not a recognized medical term and warns that it revives the false idea that autism comes from parenting ([autismus Deutschland](/research/studies/autismus-deutschland-statement/) [VERIFY]).
- **Journals, 2024 onward.** Reviews and commentaries from India, the Netherlands and Nepal conclude that the evidence is too preliminary for a diagnosis, that the link may run both ways, and that the label adds stigma for families of autistic children ([Detroja and Bhatia 2024](/research/studies/detroja-bhatia-2024/) [VERIFY]; [Krijnen 2026](/research/studies/krijnen-2026-autism/) [VERIFY]; [Dhungel 2026](/research/studies/dhungel-2026/) [VERIFY]). An autistic researcher [VERIFY identity from the paper itself] argues that research should ask how and why autistic people use media, and weigh benefits as well as risks ([van Asselt 2026](/research/studies/van-asselt-2026/) [VERIFY]).

Their shared points: the term is not a diagnosis; it risks bringing back parent blame; it blurs what autism is; and some replacement terms (such as "screen-induced developmental deviation") still assume a cause that has not been shown.

## Why a link is not proof of cause

Almost every study above is **observational**: researchers measured what families were already doing. That can show that two things go together. It cannot, on its own, show that one produces the other. Here is why, in plain terms.

**1. Reverse causation: the arrow may point the other way.**
Some babies and toddlers who will later be diagnosed as autistic may find screens especially calming, predictable or interesting, well before anyone notices other signs. Parents may also, understandably, use screens more with a child who connects with people in different ways or is hard to settle. In either case, *early autistic traits would lead to more screen time*, rather than the other way round. The Japanese genetic study is one piece of evidence that this can happen ([Takahashi N 2023](/research/studies/takahashi-n-2023-genetics/) [VERIFY]). A large Japanese cohort on general development (not autism) also found the link running both ways: lower developmental scores went with more screen time later in some families ([Yamamoto 2023](/research/studies/yamamoto-2023-jecs/) [VERIFY]).

**2. Confounding: a third factor behind both.**
Imagine a family under strain: long work hours, no extended family nearby, a parent who is unwell. That situation might mean more screen time *and* fewer chances for back-and-forth play, and it might also affect when a child gets assessed. If a study doesn't measure that strain, screens can look responsible for something the strain is really driving. Genes can be a confounder too, because autism runs in families, and parents' own traits can shape how a household uses screens.

**3. Memory and measurement.**
Many studies ask parents to remember how much their child watched years earlier, often *after* a diagnosis. Worried parents may remember differently. Most studies also use **screening checklists**, which flag children for a closer look. A high checklist score is not a diagnosis.

**4. Publication bias.**
Studies that find a striking link are more likely to be published and shared than studies that find nothing. That can make a link look stronger than it is. This is what the largest review we have found reported ([Ophir 2023](/research/studies/ophir-2023-meta-analysis/) [VERIFY]).

**5. Change over time happens anyway.**
Young children, including autistic children, develop and change quickly. When a report says a child "improved" after screens were cut, we can't tell whether that was the screens, the extra adult time that replaced them, other support, or simply growing up, unless there is a comparison group.

**What would show cause?** A randomized trial (families randomly assigned to change screen habits or not, with assessors who don't know which), or long-term studies that measure children carefully before and after, with other explanations accounted for. As of this review, we have not found a randomized trial of screen reduction and autism-like signs in young children ([Ozyazici 2026](/research/studies/ozyazici-2026/) [VERIFY]). The closest is a small Iranian study that combined a parent-child interaction programme with screen reduction; it changed two things at once, and its design is not yet confirmed ([Sadeghi 2021](/research/studies/sadeghi-2021-parent-child-interaction/) [VERIFY]).

## What we still don't know

- Whether heavy early screen time plays **any** causal part in autism-like signs, and if so, for which children.
- Whether some very young children with heavy screen use show difficulties that look like autism but are something else, and how clinicians should tell the difference. Only a qualified clinician assessing a child over time can judge that.
- Whether *when* screens are used (for example, before sleep) or *how* (alone or together, background or foreground) matters more than total time ([Lin YH 2022 letter](/research/studies/lin-yh-2022-screen-timing-letter/) [VERIFY]; [Tbilisi 2025](/research/studies/georgia-2025-bmc-pediatrics/) [VERIFY]).

## What parents can do

**1. If something worries you, ask. You don't need to wait.**
Talk with your pediatrician or family doctor about any concern: how your child talks, plays, responds, moves or connects with you. Bring notes and examples. Our [printable list of questions for your pediatrician](/research/pediatrician-questions/) can help. If your child has stopped using words or skills they used to have, tell your pediatrician soon rather than waiting for the next routine visit [VERIFY wording against CDC "Learn the Signs. Act Early." and AAP guidance; clinician reviewer to confirm].

**2. In the US, you can ask for a free early intervention evaluation yourself.**
Every US state has an early intervention program for children under 3 under federal law (IDEA Part C). You can usually contact it directly without a doctor's referral, and the evaluation is free [VERIFY against official program pages]. For children 3 and older, contact your local public school district and ask for a free evaluation [VERIFY]. [How to ask, step by step](/research/early-intervention/), with pointers for the UK, Canada and Australia.

**3. You don't have to choose between "screens" and "evaluation".**
Some families decide to change screen habits while they wait. That's fine, and if you notice changes, tell the people evaluating your child: what you see at home is useful to them. But please don't wait to see whether cutting screens makes a difference before asking for an evaluation. Support is worth having whatever the reasons behind what you're noticing, and an evaluation is how you find out what your child needs.

**4. Everyday play and conversation, as ordinary family life.**
Talking, singing, reading and playing together are good for every child, autistic or not. They are not a treatment, they are not a way to make a child less autistic, and they are not a test you can fail. A few easy ways in:

- **Pause and wait.** Leave a little gap so your child can take a turn, with a sound, a look, a gesture or a word.
- **Say what you see.** "Big ball. Rolling. Stop!"
- **Repeat and add one word.** Child: "Car." You: "Blue car."
- **Offer a choice.** "Banana or apple?" A point, a reach or a look counts as an answer.
- **Follow their lead.** Join in with what your child is already interested in, including lining things up, spinning wheels or watching the same thing again. Their interests are a way in, not a problem.
- **Sing and gesture.** Songs with actions invite turn-taking without pressure.
- **Use your home language.** Talk, sing and read in the language you know best. Every language counts.

**5. What guidelines say about screens for little ones.**
The World Health Organization recommends no screen time for babies under 1 and no more than 1 hour a day for 2-to-4-year-olds, with plenty of active play ([WHO 2019](/research/studies/who-2019-under-5-guidelines/)). For 1-year-olds, it is recalled as not recommending sedentary screen time at all [VERIFY against the guideline]. These limits are precautionary, not a line past which harm has been shown. The American Academy of Pediatrics published detailed advice for under-5s in 2016 ([AAP 2016](/research/studies/aap-2016-media-and-young-minds/)) and a new policy statement in January 2026 that reportedly replaces its earlier media guidance ([AAP 2026](/research/studies/aap-2026-digital-ecosystems/) [VERIFY]). We will summarize the 2026 advice once we have read it. Research on *how* screens are used suggests that watching together and keeping background TV off may matter as well as total time ([Mallawaarachchi 2024](/research/studies/mallawaarachchi-2024-contexts/) [VERIFY]).

**6. Go easy on yourself.**
Almost every family uses screens. Using a screen so you can shower, cook or catch your breath does not make you a bad parent. No study has shown that screens cause autism. If your child is autistic, you did not cause it, and there is nothing here to feel guilty about. Your child is not a problem to be solved.
