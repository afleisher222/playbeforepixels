# How to ask for a free early intervention evaluation

> **This is not medical or legal advice.** Programs and rules differ by state and country and change over time. Always check with your local program. Everything specific on this page is marked [VERIFY] until we have checked it against the official source.

If you're worried about how your child is talking, playing, moving, responding or connecting with you, **you can ask for help now.** You don't need to be sure anything is wrong, you don't need a diagnosis, and you don't need to wait and see. Asking early is a caring step, and it's not your fault.

This applies whether or not screens are part of the story. Early support is useful whatever the reason for a child's difficulties, and an evaluation is how you find out what your child needs.

## In the United States: children under 3

### What it is

Every US state runs an **early intervention** program for babies and toddlers from birth to age 3 who have, or may have, a developmental delay or disability. It exists under a federal law, the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, **Part C** [VERIFY].

### The key facts [VERIFY each against official pages]

- **You can refer your own child.** In general, parents can contact the program directly. A doctor's referral is not required, though it's a good idea to tell your pediatrician.
- **The evaluation is free.** Evaluation and assessment are provided at no cost to families.
- **There are timelines.** Federal rules set a timeline (commonly described as 45 days from referral) for the evaluation, assessment and first planning meeting.
- **If your child qualifies**, the family and the team write an **Individualized Family Service Plan (IFSP)**. Services are often provided at home or in other everyday settings.
- **Cost of services depends on your state.** Some services are free; some states use sliding fees or insurance. Federal rules say services can't be refused because a family can't pay.
- **A diagnosis isn't needed** to be evaluated. Eligibility is based on your child's development, and each state sets its own definition of delay.

### How to ask, step by step

1. **Find your state's program.** Use a national directory, such as the CDC's "Learn the Signs. Act Early." state contacts, or the list of state early intervention coordinators kept by the federally funded Early Childhood Technical Assistance Center [VERIFY links]. Your pediatrician's office can also tell you.
2. **Call, email or use the online form.** Say something like the script below.
3. **Write down** the date, the person you spoke to, and what they said will happen next.
4. **Follow up in writing** if you don't hear back within the time they gave you. Keep copies.
5. **Tell your pediatrician** you've made the referral, so they can share any records the program needs.

### A script you can use

> "Hello. I'd like to refer my child for a free early intervention evaluation. My child is [age in months]. I'm concerned about [how they talk / how they play / how they respond when I call their name / how they move / something else]. I'd like to know the next steps and when I can expect to hear from you. My contact details are [phone/email]."

You don't need special words. "I'm worried and I'd like an evaluation" is enough.

### What to bring or have ready

- Your child's birth date, and any health or hearing and vision check results you have.
- Notes or short examples of what worries you: what your child does, when, and how often.
- Any questions (our [printable list of questions for your pediatrician](/research/pediatrician-questions/) may help).
- If you want it, someone to come with you or join the call.

## Children aged 3 and older

In the US, children aged 3 and older can be evaluated through the **local public school district**, under **IDEA Part B** (preschool special education), even if they don't attend a school yet [VERIFY].

- Contact your local public school district's special education or preschool office and **ask in writing** for a free evaluation for special education services. Date the request and keep a copy.
- If your child is already in early intervention, the program should help plan the move to school-district services before your child turns 3 [VERIFY].
- If your child qualifies, the plan is called an **Individualized Education Program (IEP)** [VERIFY].
- Every state has at least one federally funded **Parent Training and Information Center** that helps families with these steps for free. The Center for Parent Information and Resources keeps a directory [VERIFY].

## Talking with your pediatrician

Early intervention and your pediatrician work best together. Your pediatrician can:

- listen to your concerns and look at your child's development,
- use developmental and autism screening questionnaires at routine visits (US pediatric guidance recommends developmental screening at set ages and autism screening at 18 and 24 months [VERIFY against current AAP guidance]),
- check hearing and vision,
- refer you to specialists for a full assessment if needed.

**A screening result is not a diagnosis.** A positive screen means "let's look more closely". A clear screen with ongoing worries is still worth raising again.

## Outside the US

We can only give general pointers here. Please check your local services. [VERIFY all]

### United Kingdom

- Start with your **health visitor** or **GP**. In England, young children are offered routine health and development reviews, including one at around 2 to 2½ years, as part of the Healthy Child Programme [VERIFY].
- They can refer your child to local child development, speech and language, or community paediatric services. In many areas, parents can also **self-refer** to speech and language services [VERIFY by area].
- In England, each local authority publishes a **"Local Offer"** describing support for children with special educational needs and disabilities [VERIFY]. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own systems [VERIFY].

### Canada

- Start with your **family doctor, pediatrician or public health nurse**.
- Services are run by each **province or territory**, and many offer free preschool speech and language or early-years developmental services that accept **parent self-referral** [VERIFY by province/territory].
- Your provincial or territorial health or children's services website will list what is available [VERIFY].

### Australia

- Start with your **GP** or your **maternal and child health / child and family health nurse** [VERIFY state names].
- The **NDIS early childhood approach** supports young children with developmental concerns, and a diagnosis is not needed to ask for help. Check the current age range and how to contact an early childhood partner on the NDIS website [VERIFY].

### Everywhere else

Ask your doctor, nurse or health visitor about free local early support, and whether you can refer your child yourself.

## Common worries

**"What if I'm overreacting?"** Evaluators would much rather see a child who turns out to be fine than miss a child who needs support. Asking is never wrong.

**"What if they say it's autism?"** Then you'll know more about how your child experiences the world, and you can get support that fits them. Autism is not a parent's fault, and autistic children grow, learn and thrive with understanding and the right help.

**"We've had a lot of screen time. Will they judge us?"** Nearly every family uses screens. Evaluators are there to understand your child, not to judge you. It's fine to mention screen habits honestly; it helps the team see the whole picture.
