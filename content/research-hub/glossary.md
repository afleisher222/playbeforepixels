# Glossary

Plain-language meanings of words you'll meet on our research pages. Where it helps, we point to a study that shows the idea in action.

> "Virtual autism" is a term some clinicians use for autism-like behaviors seen in some young children with heavy early screen exposure; it is not a medical diagnosis. Studies show links, not proof of cause.

## Words about the debate

**"Virtual autism."** A term some clinicians use for autism-like behaviors reported in some young children with heavy early screen exposure. It is **not a medical diagnosis** and is not in the ICD or DSM. Coined in Romania in 2018 ([Zamfir 2018](/research/studies/zamfir-2018/) [VERIFY]) and proposed in the research literature in 2019 ([Harlé 2019](/research/studies/harle-2019/)). Also seen as "autisme virtuel" (French), "autismul virtual" (Romanian), "virtueller Autismus" (German), "sanal otizm" (Turkish) and "autismo virtual" (Spanish and Portuguese).

**"Digital autism."** Another informal label for the same idea. Not a diagnosis.

**EPEE (Exposition précoce et excessive aux écrans).** "Early and excessive screen exposure": a set of signs proposed by French clinicians in 2018. The name deliberately avoids the word autism. Not a recognized diagnosis ([Marcelli 2018](/research/studies/marcelli-2018-epee/) [VERIFY]).

**Electronic Screen Syndrome (ESS).** A label from a popular book describing a proposed problem with mood, attention and arousal linked to interactive screens. Broader than autism-like signs. Not a recognized diagnosis ([Dunckley](/research/studies/dunckley-electronic-screen-syndrome/) [VERIFY]).

**Post-Digital Nannying Autism Syndrome (PDNAS).** A label proposed by one Iranian research group. Not a recognized diagnosis ([Pouretemad 2022](/research/studies/pouretemad-2022-pdnas/) [VERIFY]).

**"Screen-related developmental delay."** A more neutral term suggested by autism researchers in place of "virtual autism" ([Krijnen 2026](/research/studies/krijnen-2026-autism/) [VERIFY]).

**"Screen-induced developmental deviation."** A research term proposed by clinicians in Nepal. Note that "screen-induced" assumes a cause that has not been shown ([Dhungel 2026](/research/studies/dhungel-2026/) [VERIFY]).

**Parent blame.** The old, false idea that autism is produced by how parents raise a child. Labels that put the cause in a family's home life, such as "digital nannying", can carry the same message even when they are about screens. Autism organizations and researchers warn that "virtual autism" risks reviving it ([autismus Deutschland](/research/studies/autismus-deutschland-statement/) [VERIFY]).

## Words about autism and development

**Autism (autism spectrum disorder, ASD).** A lifelong neurodevelopmental difference that affects how a person communicates, relates to others and experiences the world, diagnosed by qualified clinicians using established criteria [VERIFY definition against WHO ICD-11 or DSM-5-TR wording]. "ASD" is the clinical term used in most studies. We say plain "autism" and use "ASD" only when quoting a study, because "disorder" frames a way of being as an illness.

**Autistic.** Many autistic people prefer identity-first language ("autistic child") because they see autism as part of who they are. Others prefer "child with autism". We mostly use identity-first language and respect each person's choice.

**Autism-like behaviors / "autism-like symptoms".** Behaviors that resemble some features of autism, usually measured with a checklist. Having some of them does not mean a child is autistic.

**Autistic traits.** Ways of communicating, playing, moving or sensing that are common in autistic people, such as intense interests, repetitive movements or a preference for sameness. Many studies call these "symptoms". We say "traits" or "signs", because they are part of how a person experiences the world, not signs of illness.

**"Severity" scores.** Some checklists and assessments give a "severity" score: roughly, how many autistic traits were observed, and how strongly, on that tool. We use the word only in quotation marks. A higher score is not a measure of a child's well-being, happiness or worth, and a lower score after some change does not mean a child is better off.

**"Risk" and "likelihood".** Studies and screening tools often talk about "risk of autism" or a "high-risk" screening score. In research, "risk" just means the chance of an outcome. Because autism is a difference and not a danger, we prefer "likelihood" (for example, "increased likelihood of autism"), a word some autism researchers also use ([Hill 2020](/research/studies/hill-2020/) [VERIFY]). When a study or tool uses "risk", we quote it.

**Neurodiversity / neurodiversity-affirming.** The idea that brains naturally differ, and an approach that respects autistic and other neurodivergent ways of being rather than trying to make people "normal" ([van Asselt 2026](/research/studies/van-asselt-2026/) [VERIFY]).

**Developmental delay.** When a child reaches skills (talking, moving, playing, problem-solving) later than most children the same age. It has many possible reasons and is not the same as autism.

**Loss of skills (regression).** When a child stops using words or skills they used to have. At any age, it is a reason to tell your pediatrician soon rather than waiting for the next routine visit [VERIFY wording against CDC and AAP guidance].

**AAC (augmentative and alternative communication).** Ways to communicate besides speech, including picture boards and speech-generating apps on tablets. An AAC device is a communication tool, not "screen time".

**Conversational turns.** Back-and-forth exchanges between a child and an adult: the child babbles, the adult answers, the child answers again. Counted in home recordings ([Brushe 2024](/research/studies/brushe-2024/)).

**Co-viewing.** Watching a screen together with a caregiver who talks about it. Associated with stronger language in reviews ([Madigan 2020](/research/studies/madigan-2020-language-meta-analysis/) [VERIFY]).

**Background TV.** A TV on in the room when no one is really watching. Associated with weaker language in reviews ([Madigan 2020](/research/studies/madigan-2020-language-meta-analysis/) [VERIFY]).

## Tests and checklists

**Screening vs. diagnosis.** A **screening** tool is a short questionnaire that flags children who may need a closer look. A **diagnosis** comes from a full assessment by a qualified clinician. Most studies on our hub use screening tools, not diagnoses.

**M-CHAT-R/F (Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised with Follow-Up).** A parent questionnaire used to screen toddlers for signs that suggest a fuller autism assessment [VERIFY]. Its results are grouped as low, medium or high "risk" (see "Risk" and "likelihood" above). A positive result is not a diagnosis ([Sundarimaa 2025](/research/studies/sundarimaa-2025-singapore/) [VERIFY]).

**ADOS (Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule).** A structured, play-based assessment used by trained clinicians as part of an autism evaluation [VERIFY].

**CARS (Childhood Autism Rating Scale).** A rating scale clinicians use to describe autism-related behaviors [VERIFY].

**SCQ (Social Communication Questionnaire).** A parent questionnaire about social communication, used for screening [VERIFY].

**ASQ-3 (Ages and Stages Questionnaires, Third Edition).** A parent-completed developmental screening questionnaire covering areas like communication, problem-solving and motor skills [VERIFY]. Not an autism test ([Takahashi I 2023](/research/studies/takahashi-i-2023/)).

## How studies are designed

**Case report / case series.** A description of one child or a small group. Useful for raising questions; cannot show cause ([Heffler 2022](/research/studies/heffler-2022-case-report/) [VERIFY]).

**Cross-sectional study.** Measures everything at one point in time. Can show that two things go together, not which came first ([Sundarimaa 2025](/research/studies/sundarimaa-2025-singapore/) [VERIFY]).

**Case-control study.** Compares children who have a condition (cases) with children who don't (controls), often asking about the past. Vulnerable to memory bias ([Chonchaiya 2011](/research/studies/chonchaiya-2011/) [VERIFY]).

**Cohort study.** Follows a group over time. Better than a one-time survey for knowing what came first, but still observational ([Kushima 2022](/research/studies/kushima-2022/)).

**Observational study.** Researchers measure what people already do, without assigning anything. Most research on screens and autism is observational.

**Randomized controlled trial (RCT).** Participants are assigned by chance to one approach or another, which balances other factors between groups. The strongest way to test cause. We have not found one that tests screen reduction and autism-like signs in young children ([Ozyazici 2026](/research/studies/ozyazici-2026/) [VERIFY]).

**Blinding.** When the people measuring the outcome don't know which group a child is in. Without it, expectations can colour the results.

**Systematic review.** A review that searches for all relevant studies using stated rules, so it is less likely to cherry-pick ([Slobodin 2019](/research/studies/slobodin-2019-review/) [VERIFY]).

**Meta-analysis.** A systematic review that combines the numbers from many studies into one estimate ([Ophir 2023](/research/studies/ophir-2023-meta-analysis/) [VERIFY]).

**Narrative review.** An expert's overview of a topic without a systematic search. Useful, but more open to the author's views.

**Hypothesis paper.** A paper that proposes an idea to be tested, rather than testing it ([Harlé 2019](/research/studies/harle-2019/)).

**Grey literature.** Material not published in peer-reviewed journals: web posts, handouts, dissertations, reports.

**Peer review.** Checking of a paper by independent experts before a journal publishes it. It reduces errors but doesn't guarantee a study is right.

**Preprint.** A paper shared before peer review ([example](/research/studies/authorea-2025-preprint/)).

**Letter / commentary.** A short published response or opinion, usually without new data ([Alper 2020](/research/studies/alper-2020-letter/) [VERIFY]).

## Words about cause and chance

**Association (link, correlation).** Two things tend to occur together. It does not tell you whether one produces the other.

**Causation.** One thing actually produces a change in another. Showing it usually needs trials or very careful designs that rule out other explanations.

**Reverse causation.** The arrow points the other way from what you'd assume. Here: early autistic traits leading to more screen time, rather than screen time leading to autistic traits ([Takahashi N 2023](/research/studies/takahashi-n-2023-genetics/) [VERIFY]).

**Confounding / confounder.** A third factor linked to both things being studied that makes them look connected. Family stress, income, parents' own traits and genes can all be confounders.

**Genetic confounding.** When shared genes influence both the "exposure" (for example screen habits) and the outcome, making them look linked ([Zhang 2023](/research/studies/zhang-2023-shared-genetic-risk/) [VERIFY]).

**Recall bias.** Errors that come from asking people to remember the past, especially after something has happened (such as a diagnosis) that may shape what they remember.

**Selection bias.** When the people in a study differ from the wider population in ways that affect the results, for example families recruited online or at a specialist clinic.

**Ecological fallacy.** Assuming that a pattern across places (counties, countries) applies to individuals ([Waldman 2008](/research/studies/waldman-2008/) [VERIFY]).

**Dose-response.** When more of an exposure goes with steadily more of an outcome. A dose-response pattern makes a link more convincing, but it still doesn't prove cause. In one large study the odds rose across the lower screen-time groups and then levelled off, with similar, overlapping estimates in the two highest groups ([Kushima 2022](/research/studies/kushima-2022/)).

**Odds ratio (OR).** A way of comparing how likely an outcome is in two groups. An OR of 1 means no difference; above 1 means more likely; below 1, less likely. Some studies report the **log odds ratio**, where 0 means no difference.

**Confidence interval (CI).** The range of values the true result plausibly falls in. A wide interval means the estimate is uncertain. An interval from 1.55 to 660.8 means the study had too little data to say much ([Rangaraj 2026](/research/studies/rangaraj-2026/) [VERIFY]).

**Statistically significant.** A result that would be unlikely if there were truly no effect, judged by a conventional threshold (usually P < .05). It says nothing about how big or important an effect is, and a non-significant result is not proof of no effect.

**Publication bias.** Studies with striking or positive results are more likely to be published, which can make a link look stronger than it is.

**Trim-and-fill.** A statistical way of estimating what a meta-analysis would show if missing (unpublished) studies were included. It rests on assumptions ([Ophir 2023](/research/studies/ophir-2023-meta-analysis/) [VERIFY]).

**Instrumental variable (IV) analysis.** A method that uses a third factor, assumed to affect the outcome only through the exposure, to get closer to cause. Its results are only as good as that assumption ([Lin 2025](/research/studies/lin-2025-lsac/) [VERIFY]).

**Mendelian randomization (MR).** An instrumental-variable method that uses genetic variants as a natural experiment ([Cai 2025](/research/studies/cai-2025-mendelian-randomization/) [VERIFY]).

**Polygenic risk score (PRS).** A number that sums many small genetic differences linked to a trait or condition. It explains only a small part of any one person's likelihood ([Takahashi N 2023](/research/studies/takahashi-n-2023-genetics/) [VERIFY]).

**Pleiotropy.** When the same genes affect several traits, which can confuse genetic studies.

**Mediation analysis.** A statistical estimate of how much of a link "passes through" a third factor. In cross-sectional data it describes numbers, not proven pathways ([Chen 2020](/research/studies/chen-2020-mediation/) [VERIFY]).

**Maturation.** Children's ordinary development over time. It can make any change after a new approach look like an effect of that approach, unless there is a comparison group.

**Regression to the mean.** Things measured at an unusually high or low point tend to move back toward average next time, with or without any change in circumstances.

## Help and services

**Early intervention (EI).** In the US, services for babies and toddlers under 3 with, or at risk of, developmental delays, run by each state under IDEA Part C. The evaluation is free [VERIFY]. See [early intervention](/research/early-intervention/).

**IDEA.** The US Individuals with Disabilities Education Act. **Part C** covers birth to 3; **Part B** covers ages 3 to 21, including preschool special education through public school districts [VERIFY].

**IFSP (Individualized Family Service Plan).** The written plan made with a family if a child under 3 qualifies for early intervention in the US [VERIFY].

**IEP (Individualized Education Program).** The written plan for a child aged 3 or older who qualifies for special education services in the US [VERIFY].

**Health visitor.** In the UK, a specialist nurse who supports families with young children and does routine development reviews [VERIFY].

**Speech-language pathologist (SLP).** A professional who assesses and supports communication: talking, understanding, social communication and AAC. Called a speech and language therapist in the UK and a speech pathologist in Australia [VERIFY titles].
