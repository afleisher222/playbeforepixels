## In plain language

Researchers used the Longitudinal Study of Australian Children. Parents reported weekly screen time at age 2. In a standard analysis adjusted for sex, maternal education and family income, children with more than 14 hours a week had higher odds of an autism diagnosis by 12. The researchers then ran an instrumental-variable analysis, a method that tries to mimic an experiment using family income and maternal education. In that analysis, screen time was no longer significantly associated with autism.

## Who was studied

5,107 children (representing a weighted population of 243,046). About 145 had an autism diagnosis by age 12, per UK expert commentary [VERIFY]. How autism was recorded is [VERIFY], probably parent report.

## What was found

- Standard analysis: more than 14 hours a week at age 2 was associated with autism by 12 (adjusted odds ratio 1.79, 95% CI 1.24-2.58).
- Instrumental-variable analysis: no significant association.

## Limitations

- A short research letter with brief methods.
- The standard analysis adjusted for only a few factors (sex, maternal education and family income), so its positive result may also reflect confounding.
- The instrumental-variable method assumes family income and maternal education affect autism diagnosis only through screen time. That is doubtful, because both can affect access to assessment directly. The authors note possible residual confounding.
- With about 145 autistic children, the second analysis has limited precision. A non-significant result means the evidence for a causal effect is weak. It does not prove there is no effect.
- It looks at diagnosed autism, not the short-lived autism-like picture that "virtual autism" claims describe.
- Experts quoted by the UK Science Media Centre said the result fits autism being mainly genetic. That is their comment, not the paper's finding.

## What it does NOT show

- That screens do or do not play any role in autism.
