What is "virtual autism"? What the research says (and doesn't)

Draft status: draft: secondary sources only; not cleared to publish until every cited source is read (abstract at minimum) and the review steps in editorial-policy.md are done  ·  Last reviewed: 2026-09-28  ·  Words: 3,618 (clinician scope 871)  ·  Source file: content/research-hub/index.md  ·  SHA-256: 0855545e10e1
IDX-001

"Virtual autism" is a term some clinicians use for autism-like behaviors seen in some young children with heavy early screen exposure. It is not a medical diagnosis. Studies have found links (associations) between early screen time and later outcomes, not proof that screens cause autism, and autism is not a parent's fault. If you're worried about your child, talk with your pediatrician and ask about a free early intervention evaluation.

IDX-002

This is not medical advice.
This page explains research in plain language. It cannot tell you whether your child is autistic, has a delay, or is developing as expected. Only a qualified professional who meets your child can help with that. If something about your child's development worries you, you do not need to wait: talk with your pediatrician, and in the US you can ask your state's early intervention program for a free evaluation yourself. See how to ask.

The short version

  • IDX-004It is not a diagnosis. "Virtual autism" is not in the World Health Organization's ICD or the DSM, and even the psychologist who coined it says it is not a medical diagnosis (Zamfir 2018 [VERIFY]).
  • IDX-005Where it comes from. The evidence that uses the label is case reports, small clinic series without comparison groups, and surveys at one point in time. We have found no randomized trials, and no long-term studies designed to test whether signs change when screens are reduced (Ozyazici 2026 [VERIFY]).
  • IDX-006Larger studies find links, not causes, and not always the same link. Some large studies find that more early screen time goes with somewhat higher scores on autism screening checklists (Heffler 2020), or with higher odds of an autism diagnosis in some groups (Kushima 2022, boys only). Another large study found no simple "more screens, more autism" pattern (Melchior 2022 [VERIFY]).
  • IDX-007But the links may run the other way, or come from something else. Children with a higher genetic likelihood of autism already watch more as toddlers (Takahashi N 2023 [VERIFY]). The largest review we have found reported an overall positive link, but it shrank and stopped being statistically significant once the authors corrected for publication bias (Ophir 2023 [VERIFY]). Two further studies built to get closer to cause found no clear causal signal, though each has important limits: one rests on a debatable statistical assumption, and the other cannot test screen use in infancy (Lin 2025 [VERIFY]; Cai 2025 [VERIFY]).
  • IDX-008Researchers, clinicians and autism organizations, including autistic authors, have objected to the term. They say it risks bringing back the old, false idea that parents cause autism, and that it blurs what autism is (Krijnen 2026 [VERIFY]; autismus Deutschland [VERIFY]).
  • IDX-009What helps right now does not depend on settling the debate. If you're worried, ask your pediatrician and ask for a free evaluation. Talking, playing and reading together are good for every child, whatever the research eventually shows.

A note to autistic readers and their families Sensitivity focus

IDX-010If you are autistic, or you love someone who is, parts of this page may be hard to read. Much of the research below treats autism as something to be counted and explained, and the term "virtual autism" has been criticized for the hurt it can cause. Here is where we stand.

  • IDX-011Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental difference, not damage and not a failure. Autistic children and adults are whole people. Nothing on this page is meant to suggest that an autistic child is a bad outcome, or that autism is something to be prevented or fixed.
  • IDX-012Autism is not caused by parenting. The old idea that parents cause autism was wrong, and autism organizations and researchers warn that "virtual autism" risks bringing it back (autismus Deutschland [VERIFY]; Krijnen 2026 [VERIFY]). No study has shown that screens cause autism.
  • IDX-013Screens can meet real needs. An autistic researcher describes how screen use met their own needs for predictability, safety, autonomy and belonging, and argues that research should ask how and why autistic people use media and weigh benefits as well as risks (van Asselt 2026 [VERIFY]).
  • IDX-014A communication device is a voice, not "screen time". Tablets and phones used for AAC (augmentative and alternative communication) are communication tools. If your child uses one, keep it within reach.
  • IDX-015Research words are not our words for people. Studies use clinical terms such as "disorder", "symptoms", "severity" and "risk". We quote them when we describe what a study measured, and we don't use them to describe autistic people. The glossary explains what they mean.
  • IDX-016Language. Many autistic people prefer identity-first language ("autistic child"), and we mostly use it here. Some people and families prefer "child with autism", and that is their choice.
  • IDX-017Autistic voices belong in this debate. Some of the critiques we cite come from autistic researchers, and we list them with the same care as the clinical studies. Our editorial policy requires a paid autistic sensitivity read before any research page is published.
  • IDX-018If your child has just been identified as autistic: your child is the same child they were yesterday. Support is about what helps your child communicate, feel safe and thrive, not about making them seem less autistic. Autistic adults' own writing and autistic-led organizations are good places to learn what autism is like from the inside.

IDX-019If anything here is hurtful, wrong or missing, please tell us.

Where the term came from

IDX-020Romania, 2018. Psychologist Marius Teodor Zamfir looked back at the case histories of young children newly diagnosed with autism at two private centres. According to their parents' recollection, many had spent more than four hours a day with screens before age 3. He called this pattern "autism virtual" and proposed that it resembled childhood autism. The paper appeared in a literary-studies journal, not a medical one. The children it describes already had autism diagnoses. Read our summary [VERIFY].

IDX-021France, 2017 to 2019. In 2017, warnings from a French maternal and child health physician, including a widely shared video, set off public alarm that heavy tablet and phone use could produce an autism-like picture in toddlers (summary [VERIFY]). In 2018, three French clinicians proposed a different name, "EPEE" (early and excessive screen exposure), which deliberately avoids the word autism (Marcelli 2018 [VERIFY]). In 2019, French child psychiatrist Bruno Harlé published a hypothesis paper arguing that intensive early screen exposure might play a part in autism-like signs in some young children, and proposed the label "virtual autism" (Harlé 2019). His paper is an argument for an idea, built from case reports. It does not test the idea.

IDX-022Earlier roots. A 2008 US study noticed that rainier counties had higher autism rates and speculated about indoor TV time, but it had no information on any child's screen use (Waldman 2008 [VERIFY]). In 2016 two US researchers proposed an untested model in which heavy early audiovisual stimulation competes with social brain development (Heffler and Oestreicher 2016 [VERIFY]).

IDX-023Other labels you may see. "Digital autism", "electronic screen syndrome" (Dunckley [VERIFY]), "post-digital nannying autism syndrome" (Pouretemad 2022 [VERIFY]), and in other languages "autisme virtuel", "virtueller Autismus", "autismul virtual" and "sanal otizm". None is a recognized diagnosis. Some critics suggest neutral terms such as "screen-related developmental delay" (Krijnen 2026 [VERIFY]). See the glossary.

What studies have found

IDX-024It helps to sort the research by how it was done, because some designs can answer the question much better than others.

Case reports and small clinic series

IDX-025A few reports describe young children with heavy screen use and autism-like signs whose developmental scores changed after screen time was cut and adult time added (Heffler 2022, two children [VERIFY]; an uncontrolled series of 30 children in Pakistan [VERIFY]; a very small series in Indonesia [VERIFY]).

IDX-026These reports matter because they are where the idea comes from. But they cannot tell us why a child changed. None had a comparison group. In each, less screen time came together with more adult attention and, often, professional support, and young children develop quickly anyway. Assessors usually knew what had changed at home.

Surveys at one point in time, and case-control studies

IDX-027Many studies compare screen use in autistic and non-autistic children, or measure screen time and autism-screening scores at the same time. Autistic toddlers often started screens earlier and watched more (Chonchaiya 2011 [VERIFY]; Tunisia 2025 [VERIFY]). In 5,336 Singapore toddlers, above-average screen time went with about 24% higher odds of a positive autism screen (Sundarimaa 2025 [VERIFY]). A positive screen is not a diagnosis.

IDX-028Not every survey finds a difference: in a large US national survey of school-age children, autistic and non-autistic children had similar total screen time (Montes 2016 [VERIFY]).

IDX-029These designs cannot tell which came first. In the Tunisian study, autistic children often preferred repetitive videos and looked at the screen from the side. Those are ways of watching that fit with autism already being there.

Studies that follow children over time

  • IDX-030United States, 2,152 children. TV or video at 12 months went with slightly higher autism-checklist scores at age 2 (4.2% higher), but not with screening "at risk". Daily parent-child play went with lower scores (Heffler 2020). Independent experts criticized the crude screen measure and pointed out that no one was diagnosed (Science Media Centre [VERIFY]).
  • IDX-031Japan, 84,030 children. In boys but not girls, longer screen time at age 1 went with higher odds of an autism diagnosis by age 3. The odds were higher in each group from 1 hour a day upward, but the estimates for 2 to 4 hours and for 4 hours or more were similar, with widely overlapping ranges, so the study cannot tell whether the odds keep rising above about 2 hours. Autism was recorded only up to age 3 (Kushima 2022).
  • IDX-032France, 12,950 two-year-olds. Screen use went with very slightly higher odds of a medium screening score but lower odds of a high-risk score. There was no simple "more screens, more autism" pattern (Melchior 2022 [VERIFY]).
  • IDX-033Australia, 5,107 children. More than 14 hours a week at age 2 went with autism by 12 in a standard analysis, but not in a second analysis designed to get closer to cause, which itself rests on a debatable assumption (Lin 2025 [VERIFY]).
  • IDX-034United States, 82 toddlers, many with a family history of autism or ADHD. Children later identified with autism or ADHD concerns had more screen time at 18 months. The study cannot tell whether that screen time was an early sign or a contributor (Hill 2024 [VERIFY]).

Reviews that combine many studies

  • IDX-035The largest review we have found pooled 46 studies with 562,131 people. It found a positive link overall, but also strong signs of publication bias: studies with positive findings were more likely to be published. After a standard correction, the link was much smaller and no longer statistically significant. The authors concluded that the proclaimed association is "not sufficiently supported" (Ophir 2023 [VERIFY]).
  • IDX-036Two other reviews report a link (Yuan [VERIFY]; Liu 2025 [VERIFY]). Whether they corrected for publication bias is not yet confirmed. Another found the evidence mixed and too thin to confirm any link (Sarfraz 2023 [VERIFY]).

Studies that test other explanations

  • IDX-037Genes. In Japan, children with a higher genetic likelihood of autism already spent more time on screens as toddlers. The authors suggest long screen time may be an early sign rather than a cause (Takahashi N 2023 [VERIFY]). In older US children, shared genetic risk explained part of the link between screen time and attention and emotional problems (Zhang 2023 [VERIFY]).
  • IDX-038Natural experiments with genes. A Mendelian randomization study found no evidence that genetically predicted leisure screen time affects autism. Its genetic markers reflect mostly adult screen habits, so it cannot test infant exposure directly (Cai 2025 [VERIFY]).

Screens and talk: related research that is not about autism

IDX-039A separate body of research looks at screens and everyday talk and development. It is often mixed up with the autism question, so we keep it apart:

  • IDX-040More screen time at age 1 went with higher odds of communication and problem-solving delays on a screening questionnaire at 2 and 4 (Takahashi I 2023, 7,097 children).
  • IDX-041In home audio recordings, screen time at 36 months went with less talk. Scaled to the average amount of daily screen time in the study, that was 1,139 fewer adult words, 843 fewer child vocalizations and 194 fewer back-and-forth conversational turns a day (Brushe 2024).
  • IDX-042Background TV went with weaker language, and watching together went with stronger language (Madigan 2020 [VERIFY]; Mallawaarachchi 2024 [VERIFY]).

IDX-043These are associations too. They are not autism studies, and we never present them as evidence about autism.

What critics say Sensitivity focus

IDX-044Criticism of the term started almost as soon as the term did:

  • IDX-045Romania. A Romanian psychology site called the original study an essay lacking scientific rigor, not published in a recognized scientific journal (summary [VERIFY]).
  • IDX-046France, 2017-18. French commentators called the viral alert subjective and without scientific foundation (summary [VERIFY]).
  • IDX-047UK, 2020. Independent researchers said the 2020 US study's screen measure was crude and that no child was diagnosed (Science Media Centre [VERIFY]). A media and disability researcher published a letter calling for better research (Alper 2020 [VERIFY]).
  • IDX-048Germany. The national autism association says the term is not a recognized medical term and warns that it revives the false idea that autism comes from parenting (autismus Deutschland [VERIFY]).
  • IDX-049Journals, 2024 onward. Reviews and commentaries from India, the Netherlands and Nepal conclude that the evidence is too preliminary for a diagnosis, that the link may run both ways, and that the label adds stigma for families of autistic children (Detroja and Bhatia 2024 [VERIFY]; Krijnen 2026 [VERIFY]; Dhungel 2026 [VERIFY]). An autistic researcher argues that research should ask how and why autistic people use media, and weigh benefits as well as risks (van Asselt 2026 [VERIFY]).

IDX-050Their shared points: the term is not a diagnosis; it risks bringing back parent blame; it blurs what autism is; and some replacement terms (such as "screen-induced developmental deviation") still assume a cause that has not been shown.

Why a link is not proof of cause

IDX-051Almost every study above is observational: researchers measured what families were already doing. That can show that two things go together. It cannot, on its own, show that one produces the other. Here is why, in plain terms.

IDX-0521. Reverse causation: the arrow may point the other way. Some babies and toddlers who will later be diagnosed as autistic may find screens especially calming, predictable or interesting, well before anyone notices other signs. Parents may also, understandably, use screens more with a child who connects with people in different ways or is hard to settle. In either case, early autistic traits would lead to more screen time, rather than the other way round. The Japanese genetic study is one piece of evidence that this can happen (Takahashi N 2023 [VERIFY]). A large Japanese cohort on general development (not autism) also found the link running both ways: lower developmental scores went with more screen time later in some families (Yamamoto 2023 [VERIFY]).

IDX-0532. Confounding: a third factor behind both. Imagine a family under strain: long work hours, no extended family nearby, a parent who is unwell. That situation might mean more screen time and fewer chances for back-and-forth play, and it might also affect when a child gets assessed. If a study doesn't measure that strain, screens can look responsible for something the strain is really driving. Genes can be a confounder too, because autism runs in families, and parents' own traits can shape how a household uses screens.

IDX-0543. Memory and measurement. Many studies ask parents to remember how much their child watched years earlier, often after a diagnosis. Worried parents may remember differently. Most studies also use screening checklists, which flag children for a closer look. A high checklist score is not a diagnosis.

IDX-0554. Publication bias. Studies that find a striking link are more likely to be published and shared than studies that find nothing. That can make a link look stronger than it is. This is what the largest review we have found reported (Ophir 2023 [VERIFY]).

IDX-0565. Change over time happens anyway. Young children, including autistic children, develop and change quickly. When a report says a child "improved" after screens were cut, we can't tell whether that was the screens, the extra adult time that replaced them, other support, or simply growing up, unless there is a comparison group.

IDX-057What would show cause? A randomized trial (families randomly assigned to change screen habits or not, with assessors who don't know which), or long-term studies that measure children carefully before and after, with other explanations accounted for. As of this review, we have not found a randomized trial of screen reduction and autism-like signs in young children (Ozyazici 2026 [VERIFY]). The closest is a small Iranian study that combined a parent-child interaction programme with screen reduction; it changed two things at once, and its design is not yet confirmed (Sadeghi 2021 [VERIFY]).

What we still don't know Clinician scope

  • IDX-058Whether heavy early screen time plays any causal part in autism-like signs, and if so, for which children.
  • IDX-059Whether some very young children with heavy screen use show difficulties that look like autism but are something else, and how clinicians should tell the difference. Only a qualified clinician assessing a child over time can judge that.
  • IDX-060Whether when screens are used (for example, before sleep) or how (alone or together, background or foreground) matters more than total time (Lin YH 2022 letter [VERIFY]; Tbilisi 2025 [VERIFY]).

What parents can do Clinician scopeSensitivity focus

IDX-0611. If something worries you, ask. You don't need to wait. Talk with your pediatrician or family doctor about any concern: how your child talks, plays, responds, moves or connects with you. Bring notes and examples. Our printable list of questions for your pediatrician can help.

IDX-0622. In the US, you can ask for a free early intervention evaluation yourself. Every US state has an early intervention program for children under 3 under federal law (IDEA Part C). You can usually contact it directly without a doctor's referral, and the evaluation is free [VERIFY against official program pages]. For children 3 and older, contact your local public school district and ask for a free evaluation [VERIFY]. How to ask, step by step, with pointers for the UK, Canada and Australia.

IDX-0633. You don't have to choose between "screens" and "evaluation". Some families decide to change screen habits while they wait. That's fine. But please don't wait to see whether cutting screens makes a difference before asking for an evaluation. Support is worth having whatever the reasons behind what you're noticing, and an evaluation is how you find out what your child needs.

IDX-0644. Everyday play and conversation, as ordinary family life. Talking, singing, reading and playing together are good for every child, autistic or not. They are not a treatment, they are not a way to make a child less autistic, and they are not a test you can fail. A few easy ways in:

  • IDX-065Pause and wait. Leave a little gap so your child can take a turn, with a sound, a look, a gesture or a word.
  • IDX-066Say what you see. "Big ball. Rolling. Stop!"
  • IDX-067Repeat and add one word. Child: "Car." You: "Blue car."
  • IDX-068Offer a choice. "Banana or apple?"
  • IDX-069Follow their lead. Join in with what your child is already interested in, including lining things up, spinning wheels or watching the same thing again. Their interests are a way in, not a problem.
  • IDX-070Sing and gesture. Songs with actions invite turn-taking without pressure.

IDX-071If you'd like some ideas to play with, our free printable, Five 5-Minute Plays, has quick screen-free games for little ones. It is simply play for family time.

IDX-0725. What guidelines say about screens for little ones. The World Health Organization recommends no screen time for babies under 1 and no more than 1 hour a day for 2-to-4-year-olds, with plenty of active play (WHO 2019). For 1-year-olds, it is recalled as not recommending sedentary screen time at all [VERIFY against the guideline]. These limits are precautionary, not a line past which harm has been shown. The American Academy of Pediatrics published detailed advice for under-5s in 2016 (AAP 2016) and a new policy statement in January 2026 that reportedly replaces its earlier media guidance (AAP 2026 [VERIFY]). We will summarize the 2026 advice once we have read it. Research on how screens are used suggests that watching together and keeping background TV off may matter as well as total time (Mallawaarachchi 2024 [VERIFY]).

IDX-0736. Go easy on yourself. Almost every family uses screens. Using a screen so you can shower, cook or catch your breath does not make you a bad parent. No study has shown that screens cause autism. If your child is autistic, you did not cause it, and there is nothing here to feel guilty about. Your child is not a problem to be solved.

FAQ/research/virtual-autism/faq/

Questions parents ask about "virtual autism" and screens

Draft status: draft: secondary sources only; not cleared to publish  ·  Last reviewed: 2026-09-28  ·  Words: 2,261 (clinician scope 910)  ·  Source file: content/research-hub/faq.md  ·  SHA-256: b097a95bf143
FAQ-001

"Virtual autism" is a term some clinicians use for autism-like behaviors seen in some young children with heavy early screen exposure. It is not a medical diagnosis. Studies have found links (associations) between early screen time and later outcomes, not proof that screens cause autism, and autism is not a parent's fault. If you're worried about your child, talk with your pediatrician and ask about a free early intervention evaluation.

FAQ-002

This is not medical advice. These answers explain research. They can't tell you anything about your own child. Please take any worry about your child's development to your pediatrician or another qualified professional.

About the term Sensitivity focus

1. What is "virtual autism"?

FAQ-003It is a label some clinicians and writers use for autism-like behaviors reported in some young children who had a lot of screen time early in life. It was coined by a Romanian psychologist in 2018 (Zamfir 2018 [VERIFY]) and carried into the research literature by a French child psychiatrist's hypothesis paper in 2019 (Harlé 2019). It is an idea that is being debated. It is not an established condition.

2. Is "virtual autism" a real diagnosis?

FAQ-004No. It is not in the World Health Organization's ICD or in the DSM, which are the manuals doctors use. Even the psychologist who coined it says it is not a medical diagnosis [VERIFY]. A 2024 review concluded that the evidence is too preliminary for it to be recognized as a diagnosis (Detroja and Bhatia 2024 [VERIFY]). A doctor can diagnose autism, or a language or developmental delay, but not "virtual autism".

3. Do screens cause autism?

FAQ-005No study has shown that they do. Some studies find that more early screen time goes with somewhat higher autism-screening scores, or with more autism diagnoses in some groups (for example, in boys but not girls in a large Japanese study: Kushima 2022). But:

  • FAQ-006the largest review we have found reported an overall positive link, but it shrank and was no longer statistically significant after correcting for publication bias (Ophir 2023 [VERIFY]);
  • FAQ-007children with a higher genetic likelihood of autism already watch more as toddlers, which suggests screen use may be an early sign rather than a cause (Takahashi N 2023 [VERIFY]);
  • FAQ-008studies designed to get closer to cause found no clear causal signal, though each has important limits (Lin 2025 [VERIFY]; Cai 2025 [VERIFY]).

FAQ-009Researchers have not ruled out that screens play some part for some children. They also have not shown that they do.

4. My child watched a lot of TV as a baby and is now autistic. Did I do this?

FAQ-010No. Autism is not a parent's fault. Researchers and autism organizations have warned that the "virtual autism" idea risks reviving the old, false belief that autism comes from parenting (autismus Deutschland [VERIFY]; Krijnen 2026 [VERIFY]). Some studies suggest that children later identified as autistic may be drawn to screens early, and parents of a child who is harder to settle often lean on them more. That is a normal response. No study has shown that screens cause autism. Your child is the same child you love, and what matters now is understanding how they experience the world and what helps them thrive.

5. Where does the idea come from?

FAQ-011From case histories of Romanian children who were already diagnosed with autism (Zamfir 2018 [VERIFY]), from French clinicians' observations in 2017 and 2018 (Marcelli 2018 [VERIFY]), and from a 2019 hypothesis paper that pulled together case reports from several countries (Harlé 2019). None of these tested the idea with a comparison group. See where the term came from.

6. Is it the same as "electronic screen syndrome", "EPEE" or "digital autism"?

FAQ-012They are related labels, and none is a recognized diagnosis. "EPEE" is a French proposal that deliberately avoids the word autism (Marcelli 2018 [VERIFY]). "Electronic screen syndrome" comes from a popular book and is broader than autism-like signs (Dunckley [VERIFY]). "Post-digital nannying autism syndrome" was proposed by one research group (Pouretemad 2022 [VERIFY]). See the glossary.

About the research

7. Why do some studies find a link and others don't?

FAQ-013Studies measure different things (a yes/no TV question vs. hours a day; a screening checklist vs. a diagnosis), at different ages, in different countries, with different ways of accounting for family circumstances. Small studies with striking results are also more likely to be published. When one review corrected for that, the overall link was no longer statistically significant (Ophir 2023 [VERIFY]). And a very large French study found screen use went with slightly higher odds of a medium screening score but lower odds of a high-risk one (Melchior 2022 [VERIFY]).

8. What is "reverse causation", and why does it matter here?

FAQ-014It means the arrow may point the other way. Instead of screens leading to autism-like signs, early autistic traits may lead to more screen time: a baby who finds screens especially soothing or predictable, or a family who, understandably, uses screens more with a child who connects with people in different ways. Genetic research supports this possibility (Takahashi N 2023 [VERIFY]). See why a link is not proof of cause.

9. What about the stories of children who "got better" when screens were taken away?

FAQ-015Those reports are real experiences, and we understand why they stand out to families. But they can't tell us why a child changed. In every published report we found, less screen time came with more adult attention and often professional support, there was no comparison group, and young children change quickly as they grow (Heffler 2022 [VERIFY]; Pakistan 2023 [VERIFY]). We have not found a randomized trial that tests this (Ozyazici 2026 [VERIFY]).

FAQ-016It also helps to know what these reports measured: scores on autism checklists or assessments. A lower score is not the same as a child being happier or better off, and support for an autistic child is about their communication, comfort and well-being, not about making them seem less autistic.

10. What does the research say about screens and talking?

FAQ-017Separate from the autism question, studies find that more early screen time goes with less back-and-forth talk at home and with weaker language on screening questionnaires. In home recordings, screen time at 36 months went with less talk: scaled to the average amount of daily screen time in the study, 1,139 fewer adult words, 843 fewer child vocalizations and 194 fewer conversational turns a day (Brushe 2024). More screen time at age 1 went with higher odds of communication and problem-solving delays at 2 and 4 (Takahashi I 2023). These are associations, and they are not autism studies.

If you're worried Clinician scope

11. How would a doctor tell autism from difficulties related to heavy screen use?

FAQ-018We can't answer that for any child, and neither can a website. Only a qualified clinician who assesses your child, often over time, can. What we can say is that researchers have not agreed on any reliable way to separate a "screen-related" picture from autism, and the term itself is not a diagnosis. If you have concerns, the path is the same either way: talk with your pediatrician and ask for an evaluation.

12. Should I cut screens first and wait to see if things change before asking for an evaluation?

FAQ-019Please don't wait. You can change screen habits and ask for an evaluation at the same time. In the US, an early intervention evaluation for children under 3 is free [VERIFY], and early support is worth having whatever the reason for a child's difficulties. How to ask for an evaluation.

13. What is early intervention, and is it really free?

FAQ-020In the US, every state runs an early intervention program for babies and toddlers under 3 who have, or may have, developmental delays, under a federal law called IDEA (Part C). The evaluation is free to families. Whether ongoing services cost anything depends on your state; some use sliding fees or insurance, and services can't be refused because a family can't pay [VERIFY all against official program pages]. See early intervention.

14. Do I need a doctor's referral for early intervention?

FAQ-021In the US, generally no. Parents can usually contact their state or local early intervention program directly [VERIFY]. Telling your pediatrician is still a good idea, so everyone is working together.

15. What if my child is 3 or older?

FAQ-022In the US, you can ask your local public school district to evaluate your child for free, even if your child isn't in school yet [VERIFY]. Put the request in writing and keep a copy. See early intervention.

16. We don't live in the US. Where do we start?

FAQ-023Start with your family doctor, pediatrician, or child and family health nurse (in the UK, your health visitor). Ask what free early support is available locally and whether you can refer yourself. We have general pointers for the UK, Canada and Australia.

Everyday life with screens Clinician scope

17. How much screen time is OK for a baby or toddler?

FAQ-024The World Health Organization recommends no screen time for babies under 1 and no more than 1 hour a day for 2-to-4-year-olds, with plenty of active play (WHO 2019). For 1-year-olds, it is recalled as not recommending sedentary screen time at all [VERIFY against the guideline]. The American Academy of Pediatrics gave detailed advice for under-5s in 2016 (AAP 2016) and published a new policy statement in January 2026 that reportedly replaces its earlier media guidance (AAP 2026 [VERIFY]). These limits are precautionary. They are not a line past which harm is proven.

18. Do video calls with grandparents count?

FAQ-025Video chat is live and two-way, which is different from watching a show. The AAP's 2016 statement is recalled as making an exception for video chatting even for very young babies [VERIFY]. We will update this answer once we have read the AAP's 2026 statement.

19. Does background TV matter?

FAQ-026Research suggests it may. Reviews found that more background TV went with weaker language and poorer thinking and learning outcomes (Madigan 2020 [VERIFY]; Mallawaarachchi 2024 [VERIFY]). These are associations, but turning the TV off when no one is watching is an easy change for many families.

20. Does watching together help?

FAQ-027Watching with a caregiver who talks about what's on screen went with stronger language and better thinking outcomes in reviews of many studies (Madigan 2020 [VERIFY]; Mallawaarachchi 2024 [VERIFY]). If screens are part of your day, sitting together and chatting about them is a good habit.

21. My autistic child loves their tablet. Is that harmful?

FAQ-028That depends on your child, and it's a good question for the professionals who know them. Autistic researchers point out that screens can meet real needs, such as predictability, calm and connection, and that research should weigh benefits as well as risks (van Asselt 2026 [VERIFY]). And if your child uses a tablet or device to communicate (AAC), that is a communication tool, not "screen time". Keep it within reach.

22. What can we do at home, whatever the research shows?

FAQ-029Talk, sing, read and play together as part of ordinary family life. Not as a treatment, and not as a test. Pause and wait for your child's turn, say what you see, repeat what they say and add one word, offer choices, follow their lead, and sing songs with actions. If you'd like ideas, our free printable, Five 5-Minute Plays, has quick screen-free games for little ones, just for fun and family time.

Glossary

Draft status: draft: not cleared to publish until reviewed with the pages it supports  ·  Last reviewed: 2026-09-28  ·  Words: 2,125 (clinician scope 779)  ·  Source file: content/research-hub/glossary.md  ·  SHA-256: 2a7eb98719ab

GLO-001Plain-language meanings of words you'll meet on our research pages. Where it helps, we point to a study that shows the idea in action.

GLO-002

"Virtual autism" is a term some clinicians use for autism-like behaviors seen in some young children with heavy early screen exposure; it is not a medical diagnosis. Studies show links, not proof of cause.

Words about the debate Sensitivity focus

GLO-003"Virtual autism." A term some clinicians use for autism-like behaviors reported in some young children with heavy early screen exposure. It is not a medical diagnosis and is not in the ICD or DSM. Coined in Romania in 2018 (Zamfir 2018 [VERIFY]) and proposed in the research literature in 2019 (Harlé 2019). Also seen as "autisme virtuel" (French), "autismul virtual" (Romanian), "virtueller Autismus" (German), "sanal otizm" (Turkish) and "autismo virtual" (Spanish and Portuguese).

GLO-004"Digital autism." Another informal label for the same idea. Not a diagnosis.

GLO-005EPEE (Exposition précoce et excessive aux écrans). "Early and excessive screen exposure": a set of signs proposed by French clinicians in 2018. The name deliberately avoids the word autism. Not a recognized diagnosis (Marcelli 2018 [VERIFY]).

GLO-006Electronic Screen Syndrome (ESS). A label from a popular book describing a proposed problem with mood, attention and arousal linked to interactive screens. Broader than autism-like signs. Not a recognized diagnosis (Dunckley [VERIFY]).

GLO-007Post-Digital Nannying Autism Syndrome (PDNAS). A label proposed by one Iranian research group. Not a recognized diagnosis (Pouretemad 2022 [VERIFY]).

GLO-008"Screen-related developmental delay." A more neutral term suggested by autism researchers in place of "virtual autism" (Krijnen 2026 [VERIFY]).

GLO-009"Screen-induced developmental deviation." A research term proposed by clinicians in Nepal. Note that "screen-induced" assumes a cause that has not been shown (Dhungel 2026 [VERIFY]).

GLO-010Parent blame. The old, false idea that autism is produced by how parents raise a child. Labels that put the cause in a family's home life, such as "digital nannying", can carry the same message even when they are about screens. Autism organizations and researchers warn that "virtual autism" risks reviving it (autismus Deutschland [VERIFY]).

Words about autism and development Clinician scopeSensitivity focus

GLO-011Autism (autism spectrum disorder, ASD). A lifelong neurodevelopmental difference that affects how a person communicates, relates to others and experiences the world, diagnosed by qualified clinicians using established criteria [VERIFY definition against WHO ICD-11 or DSM-5-TR wording]. "ASD" is the clinical term used in most studies. We say plain "autism" and use "ASD" only when quoting a study, because "disorder" frames a way of being as an illness.

GLO-012Autistic. Many autistic people prefer identity-first language ("autistic child") because they see autism as part of who they are. Others prefer "child with autism". We mostly use identity-first language and respect each person's choice.

GLO-013Autism-like behaviors / "autism-like symptoms". Behaviors that resemble some features of autism, usually measured with a checklist. Having some of them does not mean a child is autistic.

GLO-014Autistic traits. Ways of communicating, playing, moving or sensing that are common in autistic people, such as intense interests, repetitive movements or a preference for sameness. Many studies call these "symptoms". We say "traits" or "signs", because they are part of how a person experiences the world, not signs of illness.

GLO-015"Severity" scores. Some checklists and assessments give a "severity" score: roughly, how many autistic traits were observed, and how strongly, on that tool. We use the word only in quotation marks. A higher score is not a measure of a child's well-being, happiness or worth, and a lower score after some change does not mean a child is better off.

GLO-016"Risk" and "likelihood". Studies and screening tools often talk about "risk of autism" or a "high-risk" screening score. In research, "risk" just means the chance of an outcome. Because autism is a difference and not a danger, we prefer "likelihood" (for example, "increased likelihood of autism"), a word some autism researchers also use (Hill 2020 [VERIFY]). When a study or tool uses "risk", we quote it.

GLO-017Neurodiversity / neurodiversity-affirming. The idea that brains naturally differ, and an approach that respects autistic and other neurodivergent ways of being rather than trying to make people "normal" (van Asselt 2026 [VERIFY]).

GLO-018Developmental delay. When a child reaches skills (talking, moving, playing, problem-solving) later than most children the same age. It has many possible reasons and is not the same as autism.

GLO-019AAC (augmentative and alternative communication). Ways to communicate besides speech, including picture boards and speech-generating apps on tablets. An AAC device is a communication tool, not "screen time".

GLO-020Conversational turns. Back-and-forth exchanges between a child and an adult: the child babbles, the adult answers, the child answers again. Counted in home recordings (Brushe 2024).

GLO-021Co-viewing. Watching a screen together with a caregiver who talks about it. Associated with stronger language in reviews (Madigan 2020 [VERIFY]).

GLO-022Background TV. A TV on in the room when no one is really watching. Associated with weaker language in reviews (Madigan 2020 [VERIFY]).

Tests and checklists Clinician scope

GLO-023Screening vs. diagnosis. A screening tool is a short questionnaire that flags children who may need a closer look. A diagnosis comes from a full assessment by a qualified clinician. Most studies on our hub use screening tools, not diagnoses.

GLO-024M-CHAT-R/F (Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised with Follow-Up). A parent questionnaire used to screen toddlers for signs that suggest a fuller autism assessment [VERIFY]. Its results are grouped as low, medium or high "risk" (see "Risk" and "likelihood" above). A positive result is not a diagnosis (Sundarimaa 2025 [VERIFY]).

GLO-025ADOS (Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule). A structured, play-based assessment used by trained clinicians as part of an autism evaluation [VERIFY].

GLO-026CARS (Childhood Autism Rating Scale). A rating scale clinicians use to describe autism-related behaviors [VERIFY].

GLO-027SCQ (Social Communication Questionnaire). A parent questionnaire about social communication, used for screening [VERIFY].

GLO-028ASQ-3 (Ages and Stages Questionnaires, Third Edition). A parent-completed developmental screening questionnaire covering areas like communication, problem-solving and motor skills [VERIFY]. Not an autism test (Takahashi I 2023).

How studies are designed

GLO-029Case report / case series. A description of one child or a small group. Useful for raising questions; cannot show cause (Heffler 2022 [VERIFY]).

GLO-030Cross-sectional study. Measures everything at one point in time. Can show that two things go together, not which came first (Sundarimaa 2025 [VERIFY]).

GLO-031Case-control study. Compares children who have a condition (cases) with children who don't (controls), often asking about the past. Vulnerable to memory bias (Chonchaiya 2011 [VERIFY]).

GLO-032Cohort study. Follows a group over time. Better than a one-time survey for knowing what came first, but still observational (Kushima 2022).

GLO-033Observational study. Researchers measure what people already do, without assigning anything. Most research on screens and autism is observational.

GLO-034Randomized controlled trial (RCT). Participants are assigned by chance to one approach or another, which balances other factors between groups. The strongest way to test cause. We have not found one that tests screen reduction and autism-like signs in young children (Ozyazici 2026 [VERIFY]).

GLO-035Blinding. When the people measuring the outcome don't know which group a child is in. Without it, expectations can colour the results.

GLO-036Systematic review. A review that searches for all relevant studies using stated rules, so it is less likely to cherry-pick (Slobodin 2019 [VERIFY]).

GLO-037Meta-analysis. A systematic review that combines the numbers from many studies into one estimate (Ophir 2023 [VERIFY]).

GLO-038Narrative review. An expert's overview of a topic without a systematic search. Useful, but more open to the author's views.

GLO-039Hypothesis paper. A paper that proposes an idea to be tested, rather than testing it (Harlé 2019).

GLO-040Grey literature. Material not published in peer-reviewed journals: web posts, handouts, dissertations, reports.

GLO-041Peer review. Checking of a paper by independent experts before a journal publishes it. It reduces errors but doesn't guarantee a study is right.

GLO-042Preprint. A paper shared before peer review (example).

GLO-043Letter / commentary. A short published response or opinion, usually without new data (Alper 2020 [VERIFY]).

Words about cause and chance

GLO-044Association (link, correlation). Two things tend to occur together. It does not tell you whether one produces the other.

GLO-045Causation. One thing actually produces a change in another. Showing it usually needs trials or very careful designs that rule out other explanations.

GLO-046Reverse causation. The arrow points the other way from what you'd assume. Here: early autistic traits leading to more screen time, rather than screen time leading to autistic traits (Takahashi N 2023 [VERIFY]).

GLO-047Confounding / confounder. A third factor linked to both things being studied that makes them look connected. Family stress, income, parents' own traits and genes can all be confounders.

GLO-048Genetic confounding. When shared genes influence both the "exposure" (for example screen habits) and the outcome, making them look linked (Zhang 2023 [VERIFY]).

GLO-049Recall bias. Errors that come from asking people to remember the past, especially after something has happened (such as a diagnosis) that may shape what they remember.

GLO-050Selection bias. When the people in a study differ from the wider population in ways that affect the results, for example families recruited online or at a specialist clinic.

GLO-051Ecological fallacy. Assuming that a pattern across places (counties, countries) applies to individuals (Waldman 2008 [VERIFY]).

GLO-052Dose-response. When more of an exposure goes with steadily more of an outcome. A dose-response pattern makes a link more convincing, but it still doesn't prove cause. In one large study the odds rose across the lower screen-time groups and then levelled off, with similar, overlapping estimates in the two highest groups (Kushima 2022).

GLO-053Odds ratio (OR). A way of comparing how likely an outcome is in two groups. An OR of 1 means no difference; above 1 means more likely; below 1, less likely. Some studies report the log odds ratio, where 0 means no difference.

GLO-054Confidence interval (CI). The range of values the true result plausibly falls in. A wide interval means the estimate is uncertain. An interval from 1.55 to 660.8 means the study had too little data to say much (Rangaraj 2026 [VERIFY]).

GLO-055Statistically significant. A result that would be unlikely if there were truly no effect, judged by a conventional threshold (usually P < .05). It says nothing about how big or important an effect is, and a non-significant result is not proof of no effect.

GLO-056Publication bias. Studies with striking or positive results are more likely to be published, which can make a link look stronger than it is.

GLO-057Trim-and-fill. A statistical way of estimating what a meta-analysis would show if missing (unpublished) studies were included. It rests on assumptions (Ophir 2023 [VERIFY]).

GLO-058Instrumental variable (IV) analysis. A method that uses a third factor, assumed to affect the outcome only through the exposure, to get closer to cause. Its results are only as good as that assumption (Lin 2025 [VERIFY]).

GLO-059Mendelian randomization (MR). An instrumental-variable method that uses genetic variants as a natural experiment (Cai 2025 [VERIFY]).

GLO-060Polygenic risk score (PRS). A number that sums many small genetic differences linked to a trait or condition. It explains only a small part of any one person's likelihood (Takahashi N 2023 [VERIFY]).

GLO-061Pleiotropy. When the same genes affect several traits, which can confuse genetic studies.

GLO-062Mediation analysis. A statistical estimate of how much of a link "passes through" a third factor. In cross-sectional data it describes numbers, not proven pathways (Chen 2020 [VERIFY]).

GLO-063Maturation. Children's ordinary development over time. It can make any change after a new approach look like an effect of that approach, unless there is a comparison group.

GLO-064Regression to the mean. Things measured at an unusually high or low point tend to move back toward average next time, with or without any change in circumstances.

GLO-066IDEA. The US Individuals with Disabilities Education Act. Part C covers birth to 3; Part B covers ages 3 to 21, including preschool special education through public school districts [VERIFY].

GLO-067IFSP (Individualized Family Service Plan). The written plan made with a family if a child under 3 qualifies for early intervention in the US [VERIFY].

GLO-068IEP (Individualized Education Program). The written plan for a child aged 3 or older who qualifies for special education services in the US [VERIFY].

How to ask for a free early intervention evaluation

Draft status: draft: every program fact is [VERIFY]; official program pages could not be opened on 2026-09-28  ·  Last reviewed: 2026-09-28  ·  Words: 1,293 (clinician scope 1,293)  ·  Source file: content/research-hub/early-intervention.md  ·  SHA-256: 553e7a9e200a
EIV-001

This is not medical or legal advice. Programs and rules differ by state and country and change over time. Always check with your local program. Everything specific on this page is marked [VERIFY] until we have checked it against the official source.

EIV-002If you're worried about how your child is talking, playing, moving, responding or connecting with you, you can ask for help now. You don't need to be sure anything is wrong, you don't need a diagnosis, and you don't need to wait and see. Asking early is a caring step, and it's not your fault.

EIV-003This applies whether or not screens are part of the story. Support is useful whatever the reasons behind what you're noticing, and an evaluation is how you find out what your child needs.

In the United States: children under 3

What it is

EIV-004Every US state runs an early intervention program for babies and toddlers from birth to age 3 who have, or may have, a developmental delay or disability. It exists under a federal law, the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, Part C [VERIFY].

The key facts [VERIFY each against official pages]

  • EIV-005You can refer your own child. In general, parents can contact the program directly. A doctor's referral is not required, though it's a good idea to tell your pediatrician.
  • EIV-006The evaluation is free. Evaluation and assessment are provided at no cost to families.
  • EIV-007There are timelines. Federal rules set a timeline (commonly described as 45 days from referral) for the evaluation, assessment and first planning meeting.
  • EIV-008If your child qualifies, the family and the team write an Individualized Family Service Plan (IFSP). Services are often provided at home or in other everyday settings.
  • EIV-009Cost of services depends on your state. Some services are free; some states use sliding fees or insurance. Federal rules say services can't be refused because a family can't pay.
  • EIV-010A diagnosis isn't needed to be evaluated. Eligibility is based on your child's development, and each state sets its own definition of delay.

How to ask, step by step

  1. EIV-011Find your state's program. Use a national directory, such as the CDC's "Learn the Signs. Act Early." state contacts, or the list of state early intervention coordinators kept by the federally funded Early Childhood Technical Assistance Center [VERIFY links]. Your pediatrician's office can also tell you.
  2. EIV-012Call, email or use the online form. Say something like the script below.
  3. EIV-013Write down the date, the person you spoke to, and what they said will happen next.
  4. EIV-014Follow up in writing if you don't hear back within the time they gave you. Keep copies.
  5. EIV-015Tell your pediatrician you've made the referral, so they can share any records the program needs.

A script you can use

EIV-016

"Hello. I'd like to refer my child for a free early intervention evaluation. My child is [age in months]. I'm concerned about [how they talk / how they play / how they respond when I call their name / how they move / something else]. I'd like to know the next steps and when I can expect to hear from you. My contact details are [phone/email]."

EIV-017You don't need special words. "I'm worried and I'd like an evaluation" is enough.

What to bring or have ready

  • EIV-018Your child's birth date, and any health or hearing and vision check results you have.
  • EIV-019Notes or short examples of what worries you: what your child does, when, and how often.
  • EIV-020Any questions (our printable list of questions for your pediatrician may help).
  • EIV-021If you want it, someone to come with you or join the call.

Children aged 3 and older

EIV-022In the US, children aged 3 and older can be evaluated through the local public school district, under IDEA Part B (preschool special education), even if they don't attend a school yet [VERIFY].

  • EIV-023Contact your local public school district's special education or preschool office and ask in writing for a free evaluation for special education services. Date the request and keep a copy.
  • EIV-024If your child is already in early intervention, the program should help plan the move to school-district services before your child turns 3 [VERIFY].
  • EIV-025If your child qualifies, the plan is called an Individualized Education Program (IEP) [VERIFY].
  • EIV-026Every state has at least one federally funded Parent Training and Information Center that helps families with these steps for free. The Center for Parent Information and Resources keeps a directory [VERIFY].

Talking with your pediatrician

EIV-027Early intervention and your pediatrician work best together. Your pediatrician can:

  • EIV-028listen to your concerns and look at your child's development,
  • EIV-029use developmental and autism screening questionnaires at routine visits (US pediatric guidance recommends developmental screening at set ages and autism screening at 18 and 24 months [VERIFY against current AAP guidance]),
  • EIV-030check hearing and vision,
  • EIV-031refer you to specialists for a full assessment if needed.

EIV-032A screening result is not a diagnosis. A positive screen means "let's look more closely". A clear screen with ongoing worries is still worth raising again.

Outside the US

EIV-033We can only give general pointers here. Please check your local services. [VERIFY all]

United Kingdom

  • EIV-034Start with your health visitor or GP. In England, young children are offered routine health and development reviews, including one at around 2 to 2½ years, as part of the Healthy Child Programme [VERIFY].
  • EIV-035They can refer your child to local child development, speech and language, or community paediatric services. In many areas, parents can also self-refer to speech and language services [VERIFY by area].
  • EIV-036In England, each local authority publishes a "Local Offer" describing support for children with special educational needs and disabilities [VERIFY]. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own systems [VERIFY].

Canada

  • EIV-037Start with your family doctor, pediatrician or public health nurse.
  • EIV-038Services are run by each province or territory, and many offer free preschool speech and language or early-years developmental services that accept parent self-referral [VERIFY by province/territory].
  • EIV-039Your provincial or territorial health or children's services website will list what is available [VERIFY].

Australia

  • EIV-040Start with your GP or your maternal and child health / child and family health nurse [VERIFY state names].
  • EIV-041The NDIS early childhood approach supports young children with developmental concerns, and a diagnosis is not needed to ask for help. Check the current age range and how to contact an early childhood partner on the NDIS website [VERIFY].

Everywhere else

EIV-042Ask your doctor, nurse or health visitor about free local early support, and whether you can refer your child yourself.

Common worries Sensitivity focus

EIV-043"What if I'm overreacting?" Evaluators would much rather see a child who turns out not to need extra support than miss a child who would benefit from it. Asking is never wrong.

EIV-044"What if they say it's autism?" Then you'll know more about how your child experiences the world, and you can get support that fits them. Autism is not a parent's fault, and your child is the same child they were before the evaluation. Autistic children grow, learn and thrive with understanding and the right help. Good support respects how your child communicates and what they love; it is not about making them seem less autistic. Autistic adults' own writing and autistic-led organizations are good places to learn what autism is like from the inside.

EIV-045"We've had a lot of screen time. Will they judge us?" Nearly every family uses screens. Evaluators are there to understand your child, not to judge you. It's fine to mention screen habits honestly; it helps the team see the whole picture.

EIV-046Worried? Talk with your pediatrician. Free early intervention evaluations are available for young children. Asking early is a caring step, and it's not your fault.   • POL-012We carry no ads and no affiliate links on research pages.

How we choose sources

  1. POL-013Primary sources first. We describe a study from the study itself: the abstract at minimum, and the full text wherever we can get it. Press releases and news articles are not enough.
  2. POL-014We say what we read. Every study page has a "What we read" section: full text, abstract, or secondary only. Secondary-only pages stay unpublished, or clearly marked [VERIFY], until the original is read.
  3. POL-015An allowed-citation list. A short list of sources has been checked and approved for use across our materials. Anything outside it is marked [VERIFY] until a person has read the original and logged it in our research log.
  4. POL-016Balance is a rule, not a courtesy. We look for, and give equal space to, critical, skeptical and null studies and commentary, not only studies that report a link. In our research library the critical and null work is listed first.
  5. POL-017We weigh design, not headcount. A large cohort study or a careful meta-analysis counts for more than many case reports. Each study page says how much weight we think it deserves, and why.
  6. POL-018Grey literature is labelled. Web posts, handouts, preprints and position statements appear only when they matter to the history or the debate, and they are labelled as such.

How we write about findings

  • POL-019Associations are never described as causes. We say "linked with" or "associated with". When a study's own title or conclusion claims a cause, we quote it, attribute it, and explain whether the design can support it.
  • POL-020Every study page covers limitations and "what it does NOT show".
  • POL-021"Virtual autism" is always framed as a term some clinicians use; not a medical diagnosis; associations, not causation.
  • POL-022Screening is not diagnosis. We say which one a study measured.
  • POL-023Studies about talk or general development are never presented as autism studies.
  • POL-024Numbers are quoted exactly as reported, with confidence intervals where available, and without rounding that changes their meaning.
  • POL-025No fear, no blame. We avoid alarm words and never suggest a parent caused their child's autism.

Respectful language Sensitivity focus

  • POL-026We describe autistic people with respect. We mostly use identity-first language ("autistic child"), which many autistic people prefer, and we honour individuals' and communities' own preferences. In translation we follow each language community's norms, checked with autistic reviewers.
  • POL-027We never use words like "epidemic", "fight", "battle", "suffer", "cure" or "recover from autism".
  • POL-028We never describe autism as damage, as an outcome to be prevented, reduced or reversed, or an autistic child as a worse outcome. When a study uses words such as "symptoms", "severity", "risk", "impairment" or "reversible", we put them in quotation marks, attribute them, and explain what was actually measured.
  • POL-029A lower score on an autism checklist is never described as a child "getting better". We say what score changed.
  • POL-030We don't describe parents' screen use as the cause of a child's autism, and we point out when a label (such as "digital nannying") implies it.
  • POL-031Communication devices (AAC) are communication tools, not "screen time".
  • POL-032We look for, and include with equal care, research and commentary by autistic authors and statements from autistic-led organizations, not only clinical studies about autistic people.
  • POL-033Parents of autistic children are welcome here, and so are autistic readers, including autistic parents. Our main guide includes a note to autistic readers and their families.
