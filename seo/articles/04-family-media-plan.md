# How to make a family media plan that sticks

If screen time in your house feels like a daily negotiation, with "five more minutes," "but you said," and "why does *he* get to," you don't need stricter rules so much as **clearer ones**. A family media plan is simply a short written agreement about when, where and how screens fit into your family's life. When it's written down and everyone helped make it, you spend a lot less energy arguing about it.

This guide walks you through building one for kids aged 5 to 12, one step at a time.

## Why write it down?

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that families make a family media use plan, and suggests media-free times (such as meals) and media-free places (such as bedrooms) as part of it (Media and Young Minds, 2016). That statement focuses on children under 5, but the idea works at any age: **decide in advance, as a family, instead of deciding in the moment, one fight at a time.**

A written plan helps because:

- **It's predictable.** Many kids handle limits better when they know them ahead of time.
- **It's fair.** Everyone, grown-ups included, follows the same basic rules.
- **It moves the "no" off you.** "What does our plan say?" lands differently from "Because I said so."

## Step 1: Hold a short family meeting

Keep it to 15 minutes, with snacks. Explain that you're making a plan together, not handing down punishments. Ask each person:

- What do you love doing on screens?
- What do you love doing *without* screens?
- When do we argue about screens in our house?
- What would you like more time for?

Write down the answers. When kids help write the rules, the rules feel like theirs too.

## Step 2: Choose screen-free times

Pick a few times of day that are always screen-free, for everyone. Common choices:

- [ ] Meals
- [ ] The car on short trips
- [ ] The first 30 minutes after school, for a snack and a chat
- [ ] The hour before bed
- [ ] One morning or afternoon each weekend

## Step 3: Choose screen-free places

- [ ] Bedrooms. Devices charge overnight in a shared spot, such as the kitchen.
- [ ] The dinner table
- [ ] The bathroom

A family charging station is one of the simplest habits to set up. When the phone sleeps in the kitchen, there's one less thing to negotiate at bedtime.

## Step 4: Decide how much, and what kind

There's no single "right" number of hours for ages 5 to 12 in the guidelines we cite, so decide what fits your family. Many families find it helpful to sort screen use into three groups:

| Type | Examples | How the plan treats it |
|---|---|---|
| **Must-do** | Homework that needs a device, video calls with family | Doesn't count toward the daily limit |
| **Together** | A family movie, a game played side by side | Encouraged, and scheduled |
| **Solo fun** | Shows, games and videos alone | Has a daily or weekly limit you agree on |

**By age, a rough guide to who decides what:**

| Age | Who chooses content | Where screens are used | Kid's role in the plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5–7 | A grown-up chooses or approves | Shared family spaces only | Helps pick screen-free activities; draws the plan poster |
| 8–10 | Choose together from an approved list | Shared spaces; headphones okay | Suggests limits; tracks their own time |
| 11–12 | More choice, with regular check-ins | Shared spaces; no devices in the bedroom overnight | Co-writes the plan; proposes changes at the monthly check-in |

## Step 5: Agree on "time's up"

Most screen fights happen at the end, not the beginning. Build the ending into the plan:

- **A warning.** "Ten minutes left" and then "two minutes left."
- **A natural stopping point.** Finish the episode or the level, then stop.
- **A "next thing."** Screens off leads straight into something specific: a snack, a walk, a game.
- **What happens if the plan is broken.** Keep it calm and predictable, for example "no solo screens tomorrow." Agree on it ahead of time, not in the heat of the moment.

## Step 6: Build in what you *want* more of

A plan that's all limits feels like a punishment. Add the good stuff:

- [ ] A weekly family game night
- [ ] Reading time on paper, for everyone, including grown-ups
- [ ] Outdoor time every day
- [ ] One "yes day" activity each child chooses

## Step 7: Put grown-ups in the plan too

Kids notice when adults are on their phones at dinner. Include a few grown-up commitments ("Phones in the basket during meals, including ours") and the plan will feel fairer to everyone.

## Step 8: Write it, sign it, post it

Put it on one page, have everyone sign, and post it where screens live. Then **review it once a month.** Plans need to change as kids grow, school starts or summer arrives.

## Sample plan (fill in your own)

> **Our Family Media Plan**
> **Screen-free times:** meals · car trips under 20 minutes · the hour before bed
> **Screen-free places:** bedrooms · the dinner table
> **Solo fun screen time:** ___ minutes on school days · ___ on weekends
> **Time's up:** 10-minute and 2-minute warnings, then screens go to the charging basket
> **Our "next thing" list:** snack · bike · building blocks · a card game · a book
> **Grown-ups promise:** no phones at meals; we'll ask before posting photos of you
> **We'll check this plan on:** the first Sunday of each month
> **Signed:** ________ ________ ________

## What this does not mean

A family media plan isn't about zero screens or perfect compliance. You'll have sick days, travel days and rainy weekends where the plan bends. That's fine. The plan is a home base to come back to.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

If screen use is tied up with sleep problems, big mood changes, or worries about your child's wellbeing, talk with your child's doctor. They can help you shape a plan for your child. This article is general information, not medical advice.
