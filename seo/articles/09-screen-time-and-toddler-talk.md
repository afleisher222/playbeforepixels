# Screen time and toddler talk: what studies found

Toddlers learn to talk by talking: babbling at you, pointing, trying out words and hearing you answer. So it's natural to wonder whether screens get in the way. Researchers have started to measure this directly. Here's what they found, in plain words, and what it does and doesn't mean for your family.

## The study that listened in

In 2024, an Australian research team published a study in *JAMA Pediatrics* that did something unusual: instead of relying only on parents' memories, it used small audio recorders to capture the sounds of ordinary days at home. The researchers could then estimate how much screen sound was in the child's environment, and how much talk was going on around it.

The key measures were:

- **Adult words:** how many words grown-ups spoke near the child
- **Child vocalizations:** the child's sounds, babble and words
- **Conversational turns:** back-and-forth exchanges between the child and an adult

**What they found:** at 36 months, children with more screen time heard and made less talk. On average, screen time was linked with **1,139 fewer adult words, 843 fewer child vocalizations and 194 fewer conversational turns per day** (Brushe et al., 2024).

That's a lot of little conversations. It fits with something parents already sense: when a screen is on, even in the background, grown-ups and children tend to talk to each other less.

## Other large studies

| Study | Who took part | What they found |
|---|---|---|
| Takahashi et al., 2023 (*JAMA Pediatrics*) | 7,097 children in Japan | More screen time at age 1 was linked with delays in **communication** and **problem-solving** at ages 2 and 4. The more screen time, the stronger the link. |
| Madigan et al., 2019 (*JAMA Pediatrics*) | More than 2,000 children in Canada | More screen time at ages 2 and 3 was linked with **lower scores on a developmental screening test** later. The researchers also checked the reverse direction (lower scores leading to more screen time later) and did not find it. |

## What this does not mean

- **These studies show associations, not proof of cause.** Families differ in many ways the studies can't fully account for.
- **They describe groups, not your child.** A child who watches some TV can be a great talker, and a child who never sees a screen can still talk late.
- **Occasional screen use isn't a crisis.** The findings point to a pattern over time, not a single afternoon.
- **Cutting screens is not a treatment.** If you're worried about your child's talking, get it checked. Don't wait to see whether less screen time fixes it.

## What the guidelines recommend

- **World Health Organization (2019):** no screen time under age 1; no sedentary screen time at age 1; no more than an hour a day for ages 2 to 4, and less is better. It encourages reading and storytelling with a caregiver.
- **American Academy of Pediatrics, "Media and Young Minds" (2016):** avoid screen media other than video chat under 18 months; for older toddlers and preschoolers, choose high-quality programs, keep it to about an hour a day from age 2 to 5, and **watch together** so the screen becomes something to talk about.

## Easy ways to add back-and-forth talk

You don't need a program or special toys. Try these in the moments you already have:

- [ ] **Turn off background TV** when no one is watching it.
- [ ] **Pause and wait** after you speak. Count to five in your head to give your child room to answer.
- [ ] **Say what you see:** "You're stacking. Up, up, up!"
- [ ] **Repeat and add one word:** they say "car," you say "blue car!"
- [ ] **Offer choices:** "Banana or apple?"
- [ ] **Narrate routines:** diaper changes, bath time and cooking are full of talk chances.
- [ ] **Read together daily,** even for two minutes. See our [talk-along reading tips](/learn/reading-with-babies-and-toddlers/).
- [ ] **When you do use screens, co-view:** sit together and chat about what's happening.

**A quick daily "talk audit":**

| Time of day | Is a screen usually on? | One swap to try |
|---|---|---|
| Breakfast | | Talk about the day ahead |
| Car or stroller | | Sing, or play I-spy |
| Cooking dinner | | Toddler cupboard plus a running commentary |
| Bath and bedtime | | Story, song, cuddle |

## When to talk to your pediatrician

If you have any concern about your child's hearing, understanding, babbling, gestures or words, or if your child seems to be **losing words or skills** they used to have, talk to your child's doctor. You don't need to wait for a check-up.

In the United States, every state has a free early intervention program for children under 3. You can contact it yourself, and the evaluation is free. For children 3 and older, your local public school district can arrange a free evaluation. *(Editor: link the official page checked under verify_before_publish.)* Outside the US, ask your child's doctor or health visitor about local services.
