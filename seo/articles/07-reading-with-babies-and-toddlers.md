# Talk-along reading with babies and toddlers

If you've ever tried to read a board book to a 14-month-old, you know how it goes. They grab the book, turn three pages at once, chew the corner and wander off to find a shoe. That's not a failed story time. **That's what reading with a toddler looks like.**

The secret is to stop thinking of it as "reading the book" and start thinking of it as **having a conversation about the book.** You don't need to get to the end or read every word. What matters is the back-and-forth.

## Why the back-and-forth matters

Researchers who study early talk often count **conversational turns**, the little exchanges where you say something, your child answers (with a coo, a point or a word), and you respond. An Australian study that recorded everyday family life found that at 36 months, more screen time went with fewer of these turns: on average, 194 fewer back-and-forth turns a day, along with fewer adult words and fewer child vocalizations (Brushe et al., 2024). That's an association, not proof of cause, but it points to something worth protecting: **turn-taking time with a grown-up.**

A book is one of the easiest ways to create those turns. The World Health Organization's 2019 guideline for children under 5 encourages reading and storytelling with a caregiver during quiet time, and the American Academy of Pediatrics' "Media and Young Minds" (2016) emphasizes shared, hands-on time with young children.

## Six simple talk-along moves

You don't need special training. These six moves work with any book.

1. **Pause and wait.** Say something, then stop and count to five in your head. It feels long, but that silence is your child's cue to take a turn.
2. **Say what you see.** Point and name: "Dog. Big dog. The dog is running!"
3. **Repeat and add one word.** If your child says "ball," you say "red ball!" If they say "red ball," you say "bounce the red ball!"
4. **Offer a choice.** "Is that a cat or a duck?" Choices are easier to answer than open questions.
5. **Follow their lead.** If your child is fascinated by the moon on page two, stay there. Talk about the moon. The rest of the book can wait.
6. **Sing and gesture.** Add sounds ("moo!"), actions (clap, wave) and little songs. Gestures give children who aren't talking yet a way to join in.

## Talk-along reading by age

| Age | What it looks like | Try this | Good book features |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–6 months | Your baby looks at your face and the pictures, and listens to your voice | Hold the book close, use a sing-song voice, pause after you speak so your baby can coo back | High-contrast pictures, faces, sturdy pages |
| 6–12 months | Grabs, pats, mouths and turns pages (sometimes several at once) | Name one thing per page; copy your baby's sounds back to them | Board books, one object per page, textures |
| 12–18 months | Points at pictures, may say a first word or animal sound | When they point, name it and wait: "Duck! Quack quack!" | Simple words, animal sounds, lift-the-flap |
| 18–24 months | Asks "that?" and says or signs more words; wants the same book again and again | Repeat and add one word; leave off the last word of a familiar line for them to fill in | Repetition, rhythm, everyday objects |
| 2–3 years | Talks about the pictures, joins in with favorite lines, may "read" to you | Ask "What's happening?", link to real life ("We have a red cup too!") | Simple stories, routines, feelings |

## Make it part of your day

- [ ] **Keep books where hands can reach them.** A low basket in the living room, one in the car, one in the diaper bag.
- [ ] **Choose sturdy board books** that can survive chewing and dropping.
- [ ] **Read at the same time each day**, before nap or bed, so it becomes a cue.
- [ ] **Short counts.** Two minutes of real back-and-forth beats twenty minutes of reading at your child.
- [ ] **Read it again.** Toddlers love repetition. The fifth time through is when they start filling in words.
- [ ] **Put your phone in another room.** It makes pausing and waiting much easier.
- [ ] **Invite everyone.** Grandparents, older siblings and babysitters can all use the six moves.

## Common questions

**My toddler won't sit still for a book. Should I stop?**
No. Let them move. Read while they play nearby, read in the bath, or read just one page. Many toddlers listen more than you'd think while they wander.

**Should I read every word?**
Not at all. For babies and young toddlers, talking about the pictures is often better than reading the text. The words on the page are a starting point.

**What if my child always wants the same book?**
Wonderful. Repetition helps children learn, and they love knowing what comes next. Try changing one thing each time, like a new question or a silly voice.

**Are e-books or read-aloud apps the same?**
The AAP recommends avoiding screen media other than video chatting for children under 18 months. For toddlers, a printed book on your lap keeps the focus on you and the conversation.

**We speak more than one language at home. Which should we read in?**
The one you're most comfortable talking in. The conversation matters more than the language, and many families read in more than one.

## What this does not mean

Talk-along reading is a warm, everyday habit, not a therapy or a test. Every child talks on their own timeline. There's no need to drill words or worry if your child isn't joining in yet.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

If you have any concerns about your child's hearing, understanding, babbling, pointing or talking, including a child who seems to be losing words or skills they used to have, mention it to your child's doctor. You don't need to wait for a check-up. This article is general information, not medical or speech-language advice.
