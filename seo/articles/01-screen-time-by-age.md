# Screen time by age, from birth to 5

If you've ever wondered whether you're "doing screens right," you're in good company. Most parents of little ones have handed over a phone to get through a haircut, a phone call or dinner prep. This guide isn't here to make anyone feel bad about that. It puts the two most-cited sets of guidelines in one place, in plain words, so you can make choices that fit your family.

## The short answer

- **Under 18 months:** the guidelines say to avoid screens, **except video chats** with family and friends.
- **18 months to 2 years:** if you introduce screens, choose high-quality programs and **watch together**.
- **Ages 2 to 5:** about **one hour a day or less** of high-quality content, ideally watched with a grown-up. For this age, **less is better**.

Now the detail.

## Screen time by age: the guidelines side by side

| Age | World Health Organization (2019) | American Academy of Pediatrics (2016) |
|---|---|---|
| Under 12 months | No screen time | Avoid screen media other than video chatting (under 18 months) |
| 12 to 17 months | At age 1, sedentary screen time is not recommended | Avoid screen media other than video chatting |
| 18 to 23 months | (same as above, until age 2) | If you introduce digital media, choose high-quality programs and watch with your child; avoid solo use |
| 2 years | No more than 1 hour of sedentary screen time a day; less is better | Limit to 1 hour a day of high-quality programs; watch together |
| 3 to 4 years | No more than 1 hour a day; less is better | Limit to 1 hour a day of high-quality programs; watch together |
| 5 years | (the WHO guideline covers children under 5) | Media and Young Minds covers ages 0–5; beyond that, make a family media plan |

*Sources: WHO, Guidelines on physical activity, sedentary behaviour and sleep for children under 5 years of age (2019); American Academy of Pediatrics, "Media and Young Minds," Pediatrics 2016;138(5).*

The two sets of guidelines agree more than they differ. Both say very little or no screen time for babies, both put the limit at about an hour a day for 2- to 5-year-olds, and both favor sitting with your child over handing over a device.

## What the guidelines say beyond the numbers

The hour-a-day figure gets most of the attention, but the guidelines say more than that.

**Video chat is treated differently.** The AAP makes an exception for video chatting, even for babies. Seeing Grandma's face and hearing her voice is a real conversation, just through a screen.

**Watching together counts.** For toddlers and preschoolers, the AAP recommends that parents watch with their children and help them understand what they're seeing. Talk about what's on screen and connect it to real life: "That dog is splashing. Remember the puddle we jumped in?"

**Keep some times and places screen-free.** The AAP suggests media-free times, such as meals and car rides, and media-free places, such as bedrooms. It also recommends switching screens off an hour before bed.

**Active play and sleep matter too.** The WHO guideline isn't only about screens. It says children aged 1 to 4 should spend at least 180 minutes a day in a variety of physical activities, and it encourages reading and storytelling with a caregiver when children are sitting still.

## Why the guidelines focus on the early years

Researchers have followed large groups of children to see how early screen habits line up with later development. Three examples:

- A Canadian study of more than 2,000 children found that **more screen time at ages 2 and 3 was linked with lower scores on a developmental screening test** a year or two later (Madigan et al., 2019).
- A Japanese study of 7,097 children found that **more screen time at age 1 was linked with delays in communication and problem-solving at ages 2 and 4**, and the link grew stronger as screen time went up (Takahashi et al., 2023).
- An Australian study that recorded family life at home found that at 36 months, **more screen time went with less talk**: on average, 1,139 fewer adult words, 843 fewer child vocalizations and 194 fewer back-and-forth conversational turns a day (Brushe et al., 2024).

### What this does not mean

These are **associations**. They show that two things tend to go together, not that screens caused the difference. Families with more screen time may differ in many other ways too, and the studies describe groups, not your child. They also track patterns over months and years, not one show on a hard afternoon. The most useful way to read them is simple: **back-and-forth time with a grown-up is precious, and in these studies more screen time went with less of it.**

## How to use this in real life

The guidelines are a direction, not a pass-or-fail test. Here's a gentle way to move toward them.

1. **Notice first.** For three ordinary days, jot down when screens come out. Most families find they cluster around two or three moments, like cooking dinner, the car or getting ready in the morning.
2. **Pick one moment to change.** Not all of them. Choose the easiest.
3. **Have a swap ready.** A basket of pots and wooden spoons for dinner time, a song for the car, a board book in the diaper bag.
4. **Make screens a "together" thing.** When you do use a screen, sit together, pause and talk about it.
5. **Protect the edges of the day.** Screen-free meals and a screen-free hour before bed are the two habits the AAP names that many families find easiest to keep.

## Quick checklist: screen-smart for ages 0–5

- [ ] Video calls with family: yes, and chat along
- [ ] No screens during meals
- [ ] No screens in the bedroom
- [ ] Screens off an hour before bedtime
- [ ] Under 2: screens are rare, and a grown-up is watching too
- [ ] Ages 2–5: about an hour or less a day, and slower, gentler shows over fast, noisy ones
- [ ] One "swap" ready for your hardest screen moment
- [ ] Some time every day reading or telling stories together

## When to talk to your pediatrician

This article is general information, not medical advice. If you have any worries about your child's talking, hearing, play, sleep or behavior, bring them up with your child's doctor. You don't need to wait for a scheduled check-up, and there's no such thing as a silly question. Your pediatrician can also help you set screen habits that fit your child and your family.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does background TV count as screen time?**
The guidelines focus on children watching or using screens. Background TV is worth thinking about too, because in the Australian study above, more screen time went with fewer words and fewer back-and-forth turns at home. Turning the TV off when no one is watching is an easy win.

**Are "educational" apps okay for babies?**
The AAP recommends avoiding screen media other than video chat for children under 18 months, whatever the label says. For 18- to 24-month-olds, it recommends high-quality programs watched together.

**What if we're already way over an hour?**
You're not alone, and nothing is ruined. Start with one moment of the day and one swap. Small, steady changes stick better than a big overnight rule.
