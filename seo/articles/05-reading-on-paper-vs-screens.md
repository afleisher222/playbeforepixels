# Reading on paper vs screens: what the research shows

Does it matter whether your child reads a story in a printed book or on a tablet? For many families and teachers this is a practical, everyday question: which should we use for homework, bedtime or the classroom library?

The good news is that there's solid research to guide the answer. Here's what it found, what it doesn't say, and how to use it.

## What the biggest review found

In 2018, a team of researchers led by Pablo Delgado pulled together the results of **54 studies involving more than 171,000 readers** to compare how well people understood what they read on paper versus on screens (Delgado et al., *Educational Research Review*, 2018).

Their main findings, in plain words:

1. **Readers understood text somewhat better on paper than on screens** overall.
2. **The paper advantage showed up mainly with informational text** (the kind that explains facts and ideas, like a science passage or a nonfiction book). For stories, there was no clear difference.
3. **The advantage was bigger when readers were under time pressure.** When people had to read against the clock, paper came out ahead more clearly.
4. **The paper advantage didn't shrink over the years studied.** You might expect people to get better at screen reading as screens became part of daily life, but the gap favoring paper actually grew across the studies from 2000 to 2017.

## What this does not mean

- **It doesn't mean screens make kids bad readers.** The studies measured understanding of particular texts, not whether someone becomes a reader.
- **It doesn't mean every child reads worse on a screen.** These are averages across many people. Some readers do just as well on either.
- **The review looked at reading comprehension**, meaning understanding and remembering what was read. It didn't look at every possible benefit or drawback of digital reading.
- **The review is about people reading for themselves.** It doesn't tell us about babies and toddlers listening to a grown-up read, which is a different activity (more on that below).

The honest takeaway is modest but useful: **when understanding really matters, especially with factual material, paper has an edge worth using.**

## Why might paper help?

Researchers suggest a few possible reasons. Treat these as ideas being studied, not settled facts:

- People may **skim more** on screens and read more carefully on paper.
- A physical book gives a **sense of place**: where you are on the page and in the book.
- Screens come with **distractions**: notifications, links, the pull to switch.

## For parents: paper at home

For young children, reading is also about something bigger than comprehension: **sitting close together and talking.** The World Health Organization's 2019 guideline for children under 5 encourages reading and storytelling with a caregiver during quiet time, and the American Academy of Pediatrics' 2016 statement "Media and Young Minds" favors hands-on, shared activities for young children. A printed book on your lap makes that easy. There's no screen between you, nothing to swipe, and lots of pointing and chatting.

**Paper-first ideas by age:**

| Age | Paper-first habit | Why it helps |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 | Board books your baby can hold, chew-test and flip | Lots of pointing and naming together |
| 3–5 | A nightly read-aloud from a printed picture book | Time to pause, ask questions and look at pictures |
| 6–8 | Printed early readers and library books for practice | Nothing to tap or swipe while working out new words |
| 9–12 | Paper for textbooks, nonfiction and study; a screen is fine for a quick look-up | Matches the "informational text" advantage in the research |

**A home checklist:**

- [ ] A basket of printed books in the room where kids spend the most time
- [ ] A weekly library trip, or a book swap with friends
- [ ] Printed copies of important homework reading when possible
- [ ] Grown-ups reading on paper where kids can see it
- [ ] Screens off during reading time, including the grown-ups' phones

## For teachers: paper-first in the classroom

The research gives teachers a practical rule of thumb: **when comprehension of informational text is the goal, offer a paper version.** That's especially true for timed tasks.

UNESCO's 2023 *Global Education Monitoring Report*, "Technology in education: A tool on whose terms?", makes a related, broader point. It argues that technology in schools should be used where it clearly improves learning, and it notes that good, impartial evidence about technology's added value in education is in short supply. Neither source says "never use screens." Both invite careful choices.

**Classroom ideas (no products, no platforms required):**

- **Print the close read.** For nonfiction passages that students must analyze, give them paper they can annotate.
- **Pencils out for timed reading.** If a reading check is timed, offer it on paper.
- **Classroom library time.** A daily 10 to 15 minutes of choice reading from printed books.
- **Read-aloud without the projector.** Hold the book, walk the room, and pause for turn-and-talk.
- **Family link.** Send home a simple "paper reading" log instead of a login.

**Talking with families:** when parents ask, share the research neutrally: "A large review found readers understood informational text a little better on paper, especially under time pressure, so we use paper for close reading." Keep the focus on what helps learning, never on any particular product or program.

## Quick answers

**Are e-readers the same as tablets?**
The review compared paper with screens in general. It didn't settle differences between device types, so we won't guess.

**Are audiobooks bad?**
Audiobooks weren't the subject of this review. Listening to stories together can be a lovely family activity, especially on car trips.

**My child only wants to read on a screen. What now?**
Keep it low-pressure. Offer paper for school reading and bedtime, let them choose the books, and read alongside them. Interest often follows the book, not the format.
