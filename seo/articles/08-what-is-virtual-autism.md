# What is "virtual autism"?

If you've come across the phrase "virtual autism" on social media or in a news story, you may be feeling worried, confused, or both. This page explains, as carefully and honestly as we can, what the term means, what the research does and doesn't show, and where to turn if you have concerns about your child.

> **The short version**
> - "Virtual autism" is **a term some clinicians use**. It is **not a medical diagnosis**.
> - Research has found **associations** between heavy early screen use and some later differences in children's development. **Associations are not proof that screens cause autism.**
> - If you have any concern about your child's development, **talk to your child's doctor.** In the US you can also contact your state's **free early intervention program** yourself (details below).
> - **Nothing here is about blame.** Parents of young children are doing their best in a world full of screens.

## Where the term comes from

"Virtual autism" is a term some clinicians use to describe young children who had a lot of screen exposure very early in life and who show some behaviors that can look like autism, such as less eye contact, less interest in people, or delayed talking.

In 2019, a paper by Bruno Harlé in the journal *Trends in Neuroscience and Education* put the term forward as part of a **hypothesis**: that intensive screen exposure in the first years might contribute to autism-like symptoms in some children. A hypothesis is an idea offered for researchers to test. It is not a finding, and the paper did not establish that screens cause autism.

**Important:** "virtual autism" is not a recognized medical diagnosis. Autism itself is a real diagnosis, made by qualified professionals after a careful evaluation. If a professional has diagnosed your child with autism, that diagnosis stands on its own. This term doesn't replace it or explain it.

## What studies have found

Here's what some of the most-cited studies found, with what each one measured. All of them are **observational**: they followed large groups of children and looked at what tended to go together.

| Study | Who took part | What they found | Keep in mind |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heffler et al., 2020 (*JAMA Pediatrics*) | Children in a US study, followed from 12 months to 2 years | More screen viewing at 12 months was linked with **more autism-like symptoms** at age 2. Parents reading or playing with their child every day was linked with **fewer** symptoms. | It measured symptoms on a screening checklist, not autism diagnoses. |
| Kushima et al., 2022 (*JAMA Pediatrics*) | A very large group of children in Japan | Longer screen time at age 1 was linked with a higher chance of an autism diagnosis at age 3 **in boys, but not in girls**. | An association. The reason for the boy–girl difference isn't known. |
| Takahashi et al., 2023 (*JAMA Pediatrics*) | 7,097 children in Japan | More screen time at age 1 was linked with **delays in communication and problem-solving** at ages 2 and 4, and the link grew stronger as screen time went up. | This study looked at developmental delays, not autism. |
| Madigan et al., 2019 (*JAMA Pediatrics*) | More than 2,000 children in Canada | More screen time at ages 2 and 3 was linked with **lower scores on a developmental screening test** later on. | A screening test is not a diagnosis. |
| Brushe et al., 2024 (*JAMA Pediatrics*) | Australian families, recorded at home | At 36 months, more screen time went with **less talk**: on average 1,139 fewer adult words, 843 fewer child vocalizations and 194 fewer back-and-forth turns a day. | This study measured talk at home, not autism. |

## What this does not mean

This part matters as much as the findings.

- **It does not mean screens cause autism.** Observational studies can't show cause. Other things, such as family circumstances, a child's early temperament, or how much time adults have to play, may explain part or all of the links.
- **It does not mean your child's screen time caused their development to go one way or another.** Studies describe averages across thousands of children, not any one child.
- **It does not mean reducing screens is a treatment.** No one should delay an evaluation, or skip recommended support, in the hope that cutting screens alone will be enough. Be cautious of anyone online who promises "recovery" from cutting screens.
- **It does not mean guilt.** Many families rely on screens to get through hard days. Knowledge is for making choices going forward, not for looking back with blame.

## What the major guidelines say about screens for little ones

Separately from the autism question, two widely used guidelines set out screen advice for young children:

- **World Health Organization (2019):** no screen time for babies under 1; no sedentary screen time at age 1; no more than 1 hour a day for ages 2 to 4 (less is better). Reading and storytelling with a caregiver are encouraged.
- **American Academy of Pediatrics, "Media and Young Minds" (2016):** avoid screen media other than video chatting under 18 months; for 18 to 24 months, choose high-quality content and watch together; for ages 2 to 5, limit to about an hour a day of high-quality programs, watched together.

These guidelines apply to **all** young children. They aren't about autism.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Please talk to your child's doctor if anything about your child's development worries you, for example how they respond to their name, use gestures like pointing and waving, babble or use words, play, or connect with you, **or if your child seems to be losing words or skills they used to have.** You don't need to wait for the next check-up, and you don't need to be sure something is wrong. Asking is always okay.

**Free help in the United States.** Every state runs an early intervention program for babies and toddlers under 3. You can contact it yourself, the evaluation is free, and you usually don't need a doctor's referral. For children 3 and older, you can ask your local public school district for a free evaluation. *(Editor: link the official page checked under verify_before_publish.)*

**Outside the US**, ask your child's doctor, health visitor or local child-health service how to request a developmental assessment.
